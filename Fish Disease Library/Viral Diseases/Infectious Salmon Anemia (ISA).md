## Overview

### What is Infectious Salmon Anemia (ISA)?

Infectious salmon anaemia (ISA) is a serious viral disease of farmed Atlantic salmon (*Salmo salar*), characterised by slowly developing anaemia and circulatory disturbances [1](https://doi.org/10.1128/JVI.00047-12). It is caused by infectious salmon anaemia virus (ISAV; species *Isavirus salaris*, genus *Isavirus*, family *Orthomyxoviridae*), which makes it a relative of the influenza viruses [1](https://doi.org/10.1128/JVI.00047-12)[2](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.fsi.2024.110096). ISA was first discovered in Norway in 1984 and has since been diagnosed in all major salmon farming countries, including Canada, Scotland, the United States, Chile and the Faroe Islands, causing significant economic losses [3](https://doi.org/10.1177/10406387231223290). ISA, and detection of any ISAV variant, is notifiable to the World Organisation for Animal Health (WOAH) [3](https://doi.org/10.1177/10406387231223290).

Clinical disease is essentially limited to Atlantic salmon. Other salmonids can carry the virus without developing clinical disease [4](https://doi.org/10.1111/jfd.70219): after experimental infection, ISAV replicates in rainbow trout (*Oncorhynchus mykiss*) and persists in brown trout (*Salmo trutta*) without significant mortality, so both may act as reservoirs [5](https://doi.org/10.1046/j.1365-2761.1997.00300.x). In Chile, ISAV was first detected in 1999 in farmed coho salmon (*Oncorhynchus kisutch*), where it was associated with icterus syndrome rather than classical ISA [6](https://doi.org/10.1186/1746-6148-4-28).

## Clinical Signs of Infectious Salmon Anemia (ISA)

### Common Signs

- **External Signs:**
    - **Pale gills:** A sign of severe anaemia, seen in 38% of the fish necropsied in the first Chilean outbreak [6](https://doi.org/10.1186/1746-6148-4-28)[4](https://doi.org/10.1111/jfd.70219).
    - **Eye lesions:** Exophthalmia (bulging eyes) and periocular haemorrhage [6](https://doi.org/10.1186/1746-6148-4-28)[4](https://doi.org/10.1111/jfd.70219).
    - **Skin:** Darkened skin, with haemorrhages on the abdomen and at the base of the fins [6](https://doi.org/10.1186/1746-6148-4-28)[4](https://doi.org/10.1111/jfd.70219). Some fish show jaundice on the ventral side [6](https://doi.org/10.1186/1746-6148-4-28).

- **Internal Signs (at necropsy):**
    - **Ascites:** Fluid in the body cavity [3](https://doi.org/10.1177/10406387231223290)[7](https://doi.org/10.1016/0034-5288%2891%2990017-I).
    - **Liver:** Dark, congested and enlarged, with haemorrhagic necrosis in advanced cases [3](https://doi.org/10.1177/10406387231223290)[6](https://doi.org/10.1186/1746-6148-4-28)[7](https://doi.org/10.1016/0034-5288%2891%2990017-I).
    - **Spleen and kidney:** Congested and enlarged [6](https://doi.org/10.1186/1746-6148-4-28)[7](https://doi.org/10.1016/0034-5288%2891%2990017-I).
    - **Gut:** Congestion and haemorrhage of the foregut, intestine and pyloric caeca. In the first Chilean outbreak, haemorrhagic enteritis was the most common internal lesion (64% of fish), followed by haemorrhages in the pyloric caeca (43%) [6](https://doi.org/10.1186/1746-6148-4-28)[7](https://doi.org/10.1016/0034-5288%2891%2990017-I).
    - **Haemorrhages:** Petechiae in the visceral fat and peritoneum [6](https://doi.org/10.1186/1746-6148-4-28)[7](https://doi.org/10.1016/0034-5288%2891%2990017-I).

- **Behavioural Signs:**
    - **Lethargy:** Moribund fish are lethargic [3](https://doi.org/10.1177/10406387231223290)[6](https://doi.org/10.1186/1746-6148-4-28).

**Progression of Clinical Signs**

- **Anaemia and liver lesions:** Anaemia develops slowly [1](https://doi.org/10.1128/JVI.00047-12). Liver lesions worsen as the haematocrit falls: congestion at a haematocrit of around 25, blood-filled spaces between 25 and 15, and large areas of haemorrhagic necrosis at around 10 [7](https://doi.org/10.1016/0034-5288%2891%2990017-I).
- **Mortality:** The course of disease within a population can be prolonged and lead to high cumulative mortality [3](https://doi.org/10.1177/10406387231223290). Mortality in farm outbreaks varies widely, from 0 to 50% [6](https://doi.org/10.1186/1746-6148-4-28). In the first Chilean outbreak it reached 70% and 82% in the two affected cages [6](https://doi.org/10.1186/1746-6148-4-28).

### Causes of Infectious Salmon Anemia (ISA)

**Aetiology**

- **Causative Agent:** ISAV is an orthomyxovirus whose genome consists of eight single-stranded, negative-sense RNA segments encoding at least 10 proteins. These include two surface glycoproteins: the fusion protein (F), encoded by segment 5, and the haemagglutinin-esterase (HE), encoded by segment 6 [8](https://doi.org/10.3390/v13122428).
- **Virulent and non-virulent variants:** Variants are told apart by a highly polymorphic region (HPR) in the HE gene. Virulent variants carry deletions in this region (ISAV-HPRΔ) and cause ISA, while the non-virulent ISAV-HPR0 has a full-length HPR [3](https://doi.org/10.1177/10406387231223290). HPR0 causes common, transient gill infections without clinical signs in freshwater and marine farms and in broodstock [2](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.fsi.2024.110096)[8](https://doi.org/10.3390/v13122428). All virulent variants originally emerged from HPR0. The shift to virulence is thought to be a random event that depends on how often the virus replicates, so limiting HPR0 infection within farms may reduce the risk of new virulent variants [2](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.fsi.2024.110096).
- **Target cells:** The virus binds 4-O-acetylated sialic acids and infects the endothelial cells lining the blood vessels. Released virus attaches to red blood cells, and their engulfment by phagocytes (haemophagocytosis) may contribute to the severe anaemia [1](https://doi.org/10.1128/JVI.00047-12).

**Transmission**

- **Horizontal:** Fish-to-fish transmission through the water is thought to be the main route, with the gills as the primary portal of entry [3](https://doi.org/10.1177/10406387231223290). ISAV can also enter and replicate in the skin and pectoral fins [9](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13567-015-0265-1).
- **Between farms:**
    - In Norway, outbreak sites located next to each other or run under the same farming authorisation shared virus genotypes, consistent with local horizontal spread [10](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.prevetmed.2007.12.008).
    - In Scotland, the spread of ISA in 1998-99 was linked to vessels moving fish between sites and transporting harvest, but not to vessels delivering feed [11](https://doi.org/10.3201/eid0801.010144).
    - In Norway, ship contact networks and company affiliation were both associated with the distribution of ISA cases. Ship movements and shared equipment or personnel are potential transmission pathways, and poorly disinfected ships could carry the virus over long distances [12](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.prevetmed.2025.106667).
- **Sea lice:** In experiments, the Chilean sea louse *Caligus rogercresseyi* transmitted ISAV to naïve Atlantic salmon, but the virus did not replicate in the louse, so it acts as a mechanical vector (see [[Caligidosis (Caligus rogercresseyi)]]) [13](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.aquaculture.2013.10.016).
- **Vertical transmission:** There is no evidence for it. Genotyping of 32 Norwegian outbreaks (2003-2005) did not link outbreaks to smolt suppliers or broodfish origin [10](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.prevetmed.2007.12.008). In the Faroe Islands, HPR0 variants found in Icelandic and Norwegian broodfish were never detected in Faroese salmon, despite extensive imports of ova from both countries [8](https://doi.org/10.3390/v13122428).
- **Smolt farms:** HPR0 can persist in smolt farms as "house strains", with new variants occasionally introduced from the sea, probably through sea spray. Water and air intakes therefore need high biosecurity [8](https://doi.org/10.3390/v13122428).

**Risk Factors**

- **Farm and production factors:** In Norway (2004-2017), the mean annual risk of an ISA outbreak with an unknown source was 0.7% per site. Significant risk factors were infectious pancreatic necrosis on the site (see [[Infectious Pancreatic Necrosis (IPN)]]), a stocking period longer than 2 months, high latitude, and high fish density in the first six months at sea [14](https://doi.org/10.3389/fvets.2018.00308).
- **Proximity and contacts:** Sites close to an outbreak, sites under the same company, and sites visited by vessels that move fish are at higher risk (see Transmission) [10](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.prevetmed.2007.12.008)[11](https://doi.org/10.3201/eid0801.010144)[12](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.prevetmed.2025.106667).
- **Sea lice:** Salmon infested with *Lepeophtheirus salmonis* before exposure to ISAV had significantly higher mortality, as the lice suppressed antiviral genes [15](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0209178).
- **Water temperature:** In an experimental challenge, overall mortality was significantly higher at 10 °C than at 20 °C, despite higher acute mortality at 20 °C, and the virus was cleared faster at 20 °C [16](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.fsirep.2023.100099).
- **Genetics:** Survival after ISAV exposure varied from 32% to 95% between salmon families [16](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.fsirep.2023.100099).

### Diagnosis

**Diagnostic Methods**

- **Clinical suspicion:** ISAV can be present on a site for a long time before typical signs and increased mortality appear. Often only a small share of the fish is infected, and daily mortality in affected cages can be as low as 0.05 to 0.1%, so many fish may need to be tested by PCR to detect the infection [17](https://www.vetinst.no/rapporter-og-publikasjoner/rapporter/2026/fiskehelserapporten-2025).
- **Sampling:** For virulent ISAV (HPRΔ), surveillance targets fish with typical clinical signs such as anaemia and tests internal organs (kidney and heart). Screening for HPR0 requires random gill samples [3](https://doi.org/10.1177/10406387231223290). In a vaccinated Norwegian population, skin swabs detected ISAV more often (73%) than gill swabs (41%) or heart samples (25%), and swabbing moribund fish is recommended for early detection [18](https://doi.org/10.3389/faquc.2026.1885657).

- **Laboratory Tests:**
    - **RT-qPCR and sequencing:** Positive samples are tested by RT-qPCR and the segment 6 HPR is sequenced to tell HPRΔ from HPR0, following the WOAH Manual of Diagnostic Tests for Aquatic Animals. A multiplex RT-qPCR can separate the two variants in a single reaction, which speeds up confirmation [3](https://doi.org/10.1177/10406387231223290).
    - **Histopathology and immunohistochemistry:** Used together with PCR. Immunohistochemistry shows virus in the endothelial cells of the heart and other organs [6](https://doi.org/10.1186/1746-6148-4-28)[19](https://doi.org/10.3390/v13091770).
    - **Virus isolation:** ISAV can be grown in cell lines such as CHSE-214 and SHK-1 from kidney and spleen, and confirmed by RT-PCR and indirect immunofluorescence (IFAT) [6](https://doi.org/10.1186/1746-6148-4-28).
    - **Water sampling:** Droplet digital PCR detected ISAV shed into seawater from day 2 after a bath challenge, with a peak on day 11, one day before fish started dying [19](https://doi.org/10.3390/v13091770).

- **Necropsy finding:** In the vaccinated Norwegian population, hydropericardium (fluid around the heart) was a specific predictor of ISA, although it was seen in only 13 of 952 moribund fish [18](https://doi.org/10.3389/faquc.2026.1885657).

**Differential Diagnosis**

- **Concurrent diseases:** In the first Chilean outbreak, the affected fish were recovering from [[Salmonid Rickettsial Septicaemia (SRS)]], and high mortalities from [[Amoebic Gill Disease (AGD)]] and flexibacteriosis ([[Tenacibaculosis (Tenacibaculum spp.)]]) at nearby sites added to the diagnostic confusion. Infectious haematopoietic necrosis and viral haemorrhagic septicaemia were ruled out by ELISA [6](https://doi.org/10.1186/1746-6148-4-28).

### Treatment and Prevention

**Treatment Options**

- **No treatment:** There is no treatment for ISA [9](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13567-015-0265-1).

**Vaccination**

- **Vaccines:** In Norway, salmon are vaccinated in freshwater with a multicomponent injection vaccine containing five bacterial antigens, infectious pancreatic necrosis virus and, optionally, ISA virus. About 190 million doses containing ISA virus were dispensed in 2025 [17](https://www.vetinst.no/rapporter-og-publikasjoner/rapporter/2026/fiskehelserapporten-2025).
- **Field effect:** Vaccination helps reduce disease and virus shedding, but does not prevent outbreaks: in 2025 the fish were vaccinated at 7 of the 18 Norwegian sites with confirmed ISA. In a survey of Norwegian fish health staff, 4% rated the ISA vaccine effect as poor, 32% as moderate and 25% as good [17](https://www.vetinst.no/rapporter-og-publikasjoner/rapporter/2026/fiskehelserapporten-2025). In a vaccinated population followed through a full production cycle, cumulative mortality was 10% and mostly not caused by ISA, but ISAV-HPRΔ spread from four to all seven cages [18](https://doi.org/10.3389/faquc.2026.1885657).

**Preventive Measures**

- **Regulatory control:** ISAV-HPRΔ is a listed disease in Norway and the EU (category C+D+E), and WOAH lists infection with both HPRΔ and HPR0. An outbreak triggers a restriction zone made up of a protection zone (typically 5 to 10 km radius) and an outer surveillance zone [17](https://www.vetinst.no/rapporter-og-publikasjoner/rapporter/2026/fiskehelserapporten-2025). Detection of HPRΔ can lead to movement restrictions and depopulation of infected cages and sites [3](https://doi.org/10.1177/10406387231223290).
- **Early detection and removal:** Successful control requires early detection and fast removal of infected fish. In Norway, restriction zones have had monthly inspections and sampling since 2015 [17](https://www.vetinst.no/rapporter-og-publikasjoner/rapporter/2026/fiskehelserapporten-2025).
- **Farm management:** Measures include surveillance, mandatory health reporting, rules for live fish transport, fallowing between generations, and biosecurity best practices [3](https://doi.org/10.1177/10406387231223290). Cleaning and disinfection of vessels that move fish matters, as ships can carry the virus between farms [11](https://doi.org/10.3201/eid0801.010144)[12](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.prevetmed.2025.106667).
- **Controlling HPR0:** HPR0 is probably a significant risk factor for ISA outbreaks, and several 2025 Norwegian outbreaks were linked to HPR0 in the smolt farm that supplied the fish [17](https://www.vetinst.no/rapporter-og-publikasjoner/rapporter/2026/fiskehelserapporten-2025). Biosecurity of water and air intakes in smolt farms helps keep HPR0 out [8](https://doi.org/10.3390/v13122428).

### Case Studies

**Keeping a vaccinated population in production (Norway, from 2021)**

- **Case:** In June 2021, ISA was confirmed at an open-cage site shortly after sea transfer. The fish had been vaccinated against ISA and showed minimal clinical signs. Norwegian rules at the time required culling, but the site was allowed to continue production under strict monitoring, with inspections twice a month and PCR testing of healthy, moribund and dead fish, as well as water from the cages and well boats [18](https://doi.org/10.3389/faquc.2026.1885657).
- **Outcome:** Cumulative mortality reached 10.04%, mainly from other diseases and production factors rather than ISA. However, ISAV-HPRΔ spread from four to all seven cages by the end of the production cycle, and was detected more often late in the cycle [18](https://doi.org/10.3389/faquc.2026.1885657).
- **Lessons Learned:** Vaccination can keep ISA mortality low but does not stop the virus from spreading within a site. Skin swabs from moribund fish were the most sensitive surveillance sample [18](https://doi.org/10.3389/faquc.2026.1885657).

**Re-emergence from enzootic HPR0 (Chile, 2013)**

- **Case:** After the 2007-2010 epidemic, the virulent HPR7b was replaced by a non-virulent HPR0 variant, with 15 confirmed HPR0 cases in 2010, 53 in 2011 and 40 in 2012. In April 2013, two new ISA outbreaks occurred in the Aysén Region, caused by HPR3 and HPR14 [20](https://doi.org/10.1186/1743-422X-10-344).
- **Outcome:** Both viruses were genetically related to the HPR0 already present in Chile, so the outbreaks most likely arose from local HPR0 rather than new introductions. The affected fish were also infected with *Caligus rogercresseyi* and *Piscirickettsia salmonis* [20](https://doi.org/10.1186/1743-422X-10-344).
- **Lessons Learned:** Where HPR0 is enzootic, active ISAV surveillance is needed to detect new outbreaks early, since HPR0 is a risk factor for virulent ISA [20](https://doi.org/10.1186/1743-422X-10-344).

**Speed of culling and spread between farms (Norway)**

- **Case:** Researchers used Norwegian outbreak data and a transmission model to test how the time between detecting an outbreak and culling affected the number of farms infected next [21](https://doi.org/10.3389/fvets.2019.00481).
- **Outcome:** The longer it took to depopulate the first infected cage, the more new farms became infected. The data could not show whether culling the whole farm was better than culling only infected cages [21](https://doi.org/10.3389/fvets.2019.00481).
- **Lessons Learned:** Infected cages should be culled as quickly as possible. Given the high probability of spread between cages, depopulating the whole farm remains the safest option [21](https://doi.org/10.3389/fvets.2019.00481). The same pattern was seen in Chile, where delayed depopulation may have been a key factor in the 2007-2009 epidemic [22](https://doi.org/10.3354/dao02040).

## Data Insights

### Disease Impact by Country

#### Canada

- **Incidence:** ISA was first reported in New Brunswick in 1996 [8](https://doi.org/10.3390/v13122428) and has been detected in Atlantic Canada ever since. Some outbreaks arose from isolated infections of unknown source, while others were local clusters caused by horizontal spread [23](https://doi.org/10.1111/jfd.12670).
- **Virus variants:** Phylogenetic analysis of 2012-2016 isolates points to at least three separate introductions of ISAV into Atlantic Canada, and the first North American HPR0 subtype was found in this period [23](https://doi.org/10.1111/jfd.12670).
- **Newfoundland 2020-2021:** An outbreak affected four marine farms in southern Newfoundland stocked with salmon vaccinated against ISAV. Nine ISAV variants were identified (European and North American HPRΔ, and one North American HPR0) [24](https://doi.org/10.1111/jfd.13617).
- **Management:** Culling of infected cages or farms remains the standard practice [23](https://doi.org/10.1111/jfd.12670).

#### Chile

- **Incidence:** ISAV was first detected in 1999 in farmed coho salmon, without classical ISA [6](https://doi.org/10.1186/1746-6148-4-28). The first ISA outbreak in Atlantic salmon was confirmed in June 2007 on Chiloé Island (Los Lagos Region), caused by ISAV-HPR7b [6](https://doi.org/10.1186/1746-6148-4-28). The epidemic lasted until 2010, after which HPR7b was replaced by an enzootic HPR0 variant. New outbreaks in 2013 (HPR3 and HPR14) most likely emerged from this local HPR0 rather than from new introductions [20](https://doi.org/10.1186/1743-422X-10-344).
- **Spread:** ISAV affected about 65% of the salmon farms in the Los Lagos Region and 50% in the Aysén Region, making it likely the largest ISA epidemic ever reported [25](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.prevetmed.2011.07.005). Cases clustered around the index farm, and delayed depopulation may have been a key factor in the spread [22](https://doi.org/10.3354/dao02040). Farms with several generations on site, more incoming fish shipments and larger smolts at stocking were infected sooner, while distance from an ongoing outbreak delayed infection [26](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.prevetmed.2014.01.012).
- **Economic impact:** Production fell by 64% from 2008 to 2010, and the number of salmon stocked at sea fell by 80% from 2007 to 2009. According to the Chilean salmon industry, losses were around USD 2 billion in 2010, and 40% of workers lost their jobs [17](https://www.vetinst.no/rapporter-og-publikasjoner/rapporter/2026/fiskehelserapporten-2025).
- **Sporadic outbreaks since 2010:** After the epidemic, outbreaks became sporadic. Sernapesca recorded outbreaks at 2 sites in 2013, 1 in 2014, 1 in 2015, 2 in 2016, 1 in 2017, 1 in 2020, 1 in 2021 and 1 in 2023, and none in 2024 or the first half of 2025 [27](https://www.sernapesca.cl/app/uploads/2026/01/Informe-Situacion-Sanitaria-Salmonicultura-primer-semestre-2025-1.pdf). The 2023 outbreak was at the Punta Entrada site in the Magallanes Region. The 9 cages were harvested quickly, and the virulent variant was not detected at any of the 9 neighbouring sites [28](https://www.sernapesca.cl/noticias/sernapesca-constata-ausencia-de-diseminacion-de-virus-isa-patogeno-en-magallanes/).
- **Aysén, 2026:**
    - **Izaza (ACS 20):** A positive result was notified on 17 September 2026 and identified as variant HPR5a. Sernapesca set up an infected zone and a surveillance zone (Resolución Exenta N° 2901/2026), with the surveillance zone kept for at least two years. Positive cages were harvested early [29](https://www.sernapesca.cl/noticias/sernapesca-actualiza-situacion-de-caso-de-virus-isa-en-aysen/), and harvest of the whole site ended on 14 October, followed by cleaning, disinfection and fallowing [30](https://www.sernapesca.cl/noticias/sernapesca-informo-termino-de-cosecha-en-centro-izaza-y-mantiene-fiscalizacion-de-medidas-de-control-por-virus-isa-en-aysen/).
    - **Luchín (ACS 30A):** In early October 2026, the virulent variant was detected in 1 of the site's 12 cages, without clinical signs. Sernapesca considered it a separate event, with no known epidemiological link to Izaza [31](https://www.sernapesca.cl/noticias/sernapesca-informa-deteccion-de-virus-isa-en-nuevo-centro-de-cultivo-de-la-region-de-aysen/). All cages that tested positive were harvested by 17 October, while the remaining 7 cages stayed negative and were scheduled for harvest in early November [32](https://www.sernapesca.cl/noticias/sernapesca-informa-termino-de-cosecha-de-jaulas-positivas-a-isa-en-centro-de-cultivo-luchin/).
    - **Benjamín 746 (ACS 21A):** On 18 November 2026, routine sampling in the surveillance zone set up in September found ISAV in 4 of 12 cages, without clinical signs. The site was classified as suspect while all cages were sampled to confirm the case [33](https://www.sernapesca.cl/noticias/se-detecta-muestreo-positivo-de-virus-isa-en-centro-de-zona-de-vigilancia-de-aysen/).
- **Management:** Control measures may have protected 38-60% of farms in the Los Lagos Region and 23-41% in the Aysén Region. In densely farmed areas, a control zone of at least 10 km radius may work better than the 5 km zone recommended by WOAH [25](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.prevetmed.2011.07.005).

#### Faroe Islands

- **Incidence:** The first ISA outbreaks were identified in 2000 [8](https://doi.org/10.3390/v13122428).
- **Economic impact:** The ISA crisis brought the Faroese industry close to collapse. Production fell by almost 75% between 2003 and 2006 and took about 10 years to return to 2003 levels [17](https://www.vetinst.no/rapporter-og-publikasjoner/rapporter/2026/fiskehelserapporten-2025).
- **After re-establishment:** Once the industry was fallowed and restocked, 8.1% of apparently healthy salmon tested positive for ISAV over 53 months, all of them for the non-virulent HPR0, which appeared as a seasonal, transient infection without ISA mortality. Marine sites became infected on average 7.7 months after sea transfer [34](https://doi.org/10.1099/vir.0.027094-0).

#### Norway

- **History:** ISA was first recorded in 1984 and peaked around 1990, with at least 80 new outbreaks that year. Biosecurity measures introduced in the early 1990s reduced the number of outbreaks, but ISA was not eradicated [14](https://doi.org/10.3389/fvets.2018.00308).
- **Recent incidence:** Between 2015 and 2024 there were 10 to 25 confirmed outbreaks a year (mean 15.8). In 2025, ISA was confirmed at 18 marine sites, slightly above the 10-year mean. Outbreaks occur in every month, with a possible peak in late spring and summer [17](https://www.vetinst.no/rapporter-og-publikasjoner/rapporter/2026/fiskehelserapporten-2025).

- **Geographical spread:** Since 2020, outbreaks have been confirmed in every production area except PO1 and PO13. In 2025 a large share of outbreaks was in Vesterålen (PO9), where virus probably spread between nearby sites and infected fish were moved from one site to another [17](https://www.vetinst.no/rapporter-og-publikasjoner/rapporter/2026/fiskehelserapporten-2025).
- **HPR0:** The non-virulent HPR0 is not notifiable, but was detected at 78 sites in 2025, twice as many as in 2024 (39). Several 2025 outbreaks were linked to HPR0 in the smolt farms that supplied the fish [17](https://www.vetinst.no/rapporter-og-publikasjoner/rapporter/2026/fiskehelserapporten-2025).
- **Management:** Outbreaks trigger restriction zones with a protection zone and a surveillance zone. In 2025 the fish had been at sea for just over 12 months on average when ISA was suspected, and fish were vaccinated at 7 of the 18 confirmed sites [17](https://www.vetinst.no/rapporter-og-publikasjoner/rapporter/2026/fiskehelserapporten-2025).

#### Scotland

- **1998-1999:** ISA spread over a wide area with transported fish and harvest vessels [11](https://doi.org/10.3201/eid0801.010144)[35](https://doi.org/10.3354/dao02262) and was eradicated at a cost of over £20 million [35](https://doi.org/10.3354/dao02262).
- **2008-2009 (Shetland):** ISA re-emerged and was confirmed at six sites. This time spread was mainly linked to marine water currents and stayed within a small area. Confirmed sites were depopulated within 7 weeks (mean 3.7 weeks), and the origin of the outbreak remains unknown [35](https://doi.org/10.3354/dao02262).
- **Management:** Industry codes of good practice, fewer site-to-site movements of live fish, better disinfection of vessels and processing waste, and management areas with synchronous fallowing may explain why the 2008-2009 outbreak stayed local [35](https://doi.org/10.3354/dao02262).

#### United States

- **Incidence:** ISA was first reported in Maine in 2001 [8](https://doi.org/10.3390/v13122428).
- **Spread:** In the Quoddy region (Maine and New Brunswick), where farms are close together and tidal mixing is strong, ISA recurs repeatedly. Between 2002 and 2004, new outbreaks clustered with outbreaks one tidal excursion upstream. Holding over fish from the previous year class and sharing wharves were also risk factors [36](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.prevetmed.2006.09.006).

## Conclusion

Infectious Salmon Anemia (ISA) remains one of the most serious viral diseases of farmed Atlantic salmon. Virulent ISAV-HPRΔ causes slowly developing anaemia and circulatory failure, and there is no treatment. Outbreaks still occur every year in Norway, and Chile recorded new detections in Aysén in 2026, more than 15 years after its epidemic.

Because virulent variants emerge from the widespread, non-virulent HPR0, and the virus spreads between farms through water, vessels and fish movements, control depends on early detection, fast culling of infected cages, restriction zones, fallowing and biosecurity in both smolt and marine farms. Vaccines reduce disease and virus shedding but do not prevent outbreaks, so they work best as one part of this wider approach.
