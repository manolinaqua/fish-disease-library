## Overview

### What is Saprolegniasis?

Saprolegniasis (also known as water mould disease, cotton wool disease, or winter disease) is a destructive infectious disease of freshwater fish and fish eggs caused by oomycetes of the genus *Saprolegnia* [12](https://doi.org/10.1111/jfd.12923). The primary causative agents are *Saprolegnia parasitica*, *S. diclina*, and *S. ferax*, with *S. parasitica* widely regarded as the most virulent species in salmonid aquaculture [1](https://doi.org/10.3390/jof10010057) [3](https://doi.org/10.1111/jfd.12368) [7](https://doi.org/10.3390/microorganisms12081733). Although traditionally grouped with fungal diseases, *Saprolegnia* species are oomycetes (heterokonts), more closely related to diatoms and brown algae than to true fungi. Their cell walls are composed mainly of cellulose and β-glucans, with only about 2% chitin in *S. parasitica* [21](https://doi.org/10.3389/fmicb.2019.02873).

Saprolegniasis affects a broad range of farmed and wild freshwater fish, including Atlantic salmon (*Salmo salar*), rainbow trout (*Oncorhynchus mykiss*), brown trout (*Salmo trutta*), tilapia, and catfish such as the striped catfish (*Pangasianodon hypophthalmus*), as well as salmonid eggs in hatcheries [3](https://doi.org/10.1111/jfd.12368) [4](https://doi.org/10.1111/jfd.70028) [6](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-024-80075-0) [7](https://doi.org/10.3390/microorganisms12081733) [16](https://doi.org/10.3389/fmicb.2025.1723326). *Saprolegnia parasitica* is ubiquitous in freshwater environments, and saprolegniasis affects freshwater fish worldwide [4](https://doi.org/10.1111/jfd.70028) [21](https://doi.org/10.3389/fmicb.2019.02873).

The economic significance of saprolegniasis increased after malachite green, previously the cornerstone treatment, was banned internationally in 2002 because of its carcinogenic and toxicological effects, which led to a resurgence of the disease [21](https://doi.org/10.3389/fmicb.2019.02873). Its use in fish destined for human consumption is not authorised in the European Union [12](https://doi.org/10.1111/jfd.12923), and its removal prompted an urgent search for alternative treatments [13](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0091878) [15](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.sjbs.2020.04.008). In the major salmon-producing countries, saprolegniasis causes losses of tens of millions of euros per year, and in the United States *S. parasitica* has caused financial losses of up to 50% in catfish farming [21](https://doi.org/10.3389/fmicb.2019.02873).

## Clinical Signs of Saprolegniasis

### Common Signs

- **Physical Signs:**
    - **Cotton-like mycelial growth:** The hallmark clinical sign is the appearance of white to grey, cotton-wool-like tufts of hyphae on the skin, fins, gills, and eggs of affected fish, which may spread to cover more than 80% of the body surface [6](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-024-80075-0) [12](https://doi.org/10.1111/jfd.12923). These superficial mycelial colonies are visible to the naked eye and grow rapidly under favourable conditions.
    - **Haemorrhagic ulcers:** Beneath the mycelial mat, the epidermis is destroyed and the skin develops focal haemorrhage, necrosis and ulceration; in advanced cases hyphae reach the underlying tissues [5](https://doi.org/10.1111/jfd.12175) [12](https://doi.org/10.1111/jfd.12923).
    - **Fin erosion:** Fin margins become ragged and necrotic at sites where mycelial colonisation is established [6](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-024-80075-0).
    - **Egg infection:** *Saprolegnia* is a major cause of egg mortality in salmonid hatcheries. Infected eggs develop a characteristic white mycelial halo; *S. parasitica* penetrates the intact chorion, while *S. diclina* destroys the chorion surface [3](https://doi.org/10.1111/jfd.12368).

- **Behavioural Changes:**
    - **Lethargy and loss of equilibrium:** Infected fish become lethargic and may lose equilibrium [5](https://doi.org/10.1111/jfd.12175) [12](https://doi.org/10.1111/jfd.12923).

**Progression of Clinical Signs**

- **Early Stages:** Infection begins as small, discrete focal patches of whitish mycelium at sites of prior skin damage. Injuries, prior infections by other pathogens, and stress predispose fish to infection, so saprolegniasis is often considered a secondary infection [7](https://doi.org/10.3390/microorganisms12081733). Behavioural changes at this stage may be subtle.
- **Advanced Stages:** Mycelial colonies expand and coalesce. Hyphae destroy the epidermis and can reach the underlying tissues and blood vessels, impairing osmoregulation; gill infection can cause respiratory failure, and untreated saprolegniasis may lead to death [12](https://doi.org/10.1111/jfd.12923).
- **Impact on Fish Health:** Infection is associated with changes in the skin and egg microbiome, including reduced bacterial diversity on infected eggs and increased abundance of opportunistic taxa such as *Aeromonas* on injured skin, which may further predispose fish and eggs to secondary infections [7](https://doi.org/10.3390/microorganisms12081733). Several *Saprolegnia* isolates have caused more than 90% mortality in fish and eggs [13](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0091878).

### Causes of Saprolegniasis

**Aetiology**

- **Causative Agent:** *Saprolegnia parasitica* is the primary pathogen in freshwater salmonid aquaculture. Phylogenetic analysis of the ITS region has identified at least five phylotypes of *S. parasitica* (S2–S6); phylotype S2 is overwhelmingly dominant in Scottish Atlantic salmon farms, accounting for 91.1% of all *S. parasitica* isolates recovered during epizootic periods [1](https://doi.org/10.3390/jof10010057). In Nova Scotia, both S1 and S2 strains co-circulate; S1 exhibits persistent zoospore motility and is consistently more virulent than S2 in experimental challenges, while S2 zoospores encyst and germinate more rapidly [4](https://doi.org/10.1111/jfd.70028). *Saprolegnia diclina* clade IIIA is also frequently isolated from diseased eggs and employs a distinct infection strategy [3](https://doi.org/10.1111/jfd.12368). Morphologically, *S. parasitica* produces non-septate branching hyphae, zoosporangia, secondary zoospores, encysted spores, and persistent vegetative gemmae; culture conditions significantly influence the formation of these structures [2](https://doi.org/10.3390/jof9100982).

- **Transmission Methods:**
    - **Zoospore Dispersal:** *Saprolegnia* reproduces asexually by releasing primary zoospores from zoosporangia. These encyst and release secondary zoospores, which encyst again and germinate into new hyphae; motile zoospores, cysts and germlings can all cause disease [2](https://doi.org/10.3390/jof9100982) [4](https://doi.org/10.1111/jfd.70028).
    - **Spread Between Eggs:** In incubators, the mycelium first invades dead or unfertilised eggs and then spreads quickly to neighbouring healthy eggs, often resulting in the loss of entire egg batches [7](https://doi.org/10.3390/microorganisms12081733).
    - **Environmental Reservoirs:** Most members of the genus live as saprotrophs [12](https://doi.org/10.1111/jfd.12923), and *Saprolegnia* species are ubiquitous in the natural water supplying hatcheries, where they have proven difficult to eliminate by filtering and physical treatments such as UV and ozone [13](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0091878).

**Risk Factors**

- **Environmental Factors:** Water temperature is the environmental factor most frequently linked to outbreaks, partly because host immune defences are temperature-dependent [7](https://doi.org/10.3390/microorganisms12081733). An Indian isolate of *S. parasitica* grew within 12–24°C, peaking at 20°C with no growth at 4°C, and outbreaks in caged striped catfish occurred in winter but not in summer, likely aided by low-temperature immunosuppression of the host [6](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-024-80075-0).
- **Host Factors:** Pre-existing skin damage, for example from handling, greatly increases susceptibility by providing entry points; experimental challenge models disrupt the skin before exposure for this reason [4](https://doi.org/10.1111/jfd.70028) [7](https://doi.org/10.3390/microorganisms12081733). In wild anadromous salmonids, the move from sea water to fresh water during the spawning migration alters skin physiology and increases susceptibility to skin disease [19](https://doi.org/10.3390/pathogens13030251). Notably, genetic resistance to *S. parasitica* exists among Atlantic salmon families, with heritability estimated at approximately 0.25, indicating meaningful potential for selective breeding [11](https://doi.org/10.1111/jfd.13664).
- **Farm Management Practices:** Crowding increases injuries [15](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.sjbs.2020.04.008), and dead or unfertilised eggs left in incubators are colonised first and become a source of infection for neighbouring healthy eggs [7](https://doi.org/10.3390/microorganisms12081733).

### Diagnosis

**Diagnostic Methods**

- **Clinical Examination:** The gross appearance of white cotton-like mycelial tufts on skin or eggs is highly characteristic of saprolegniasis and is sufficient for a presumptive field diagnosis [6](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-024-80075-0) [12](https://doi.org/10.1111/jfd.12923). Stereomicroscopic inspection can confirm the filamentous oomycete growth [5](https://doi.org/10.1111/jfd.12175).

- **Laboratory Tests:**
    - **Microscopy:** Wet-mount preparations of mycelial material reveal characteristic branching, non-septate hyphae and mature zoosporangia containing zoospores. Differentiation of *Saprolegnia* morphotypes based on gemma formation, zoosporangium shape, and zoospore release patterns allows species and strain-level characterization [2](https://doi.org/10.3390/jof9100982).
    - **Culture Methods:** Isolation on glucose-yeast extract agar supports pure culture growth for morphological and molecular characterisation [2](https://doi.org/10.3390/jof9100982) [5](https://doi.org/10.1111/jfd.12175).
    - **Molecular Methods (PCR/qPCR):** Validated real-time qPCR assays targeting the ITS region enable specific, sensitive detection of *S. parasitica* in both fish mucus swabs and environmental water samples (eDNA), with 100% sensitivity for *S. parasitica*-positive water samples confirmed in field validation studies [8](https://doi.org/10.3390/microorganisms10112186). Multiplex PCR protocols targeting both the rDNA-ITS region and a hypothetical protein gene allow species-level identification in a single reaction [9](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.btre.2022.e00758). Loop-mediated isothermal amplification (LAMP) offers superior analytical sensitivity compared to qPCR and is suitable for rapid, on-site diagnostics in as little as 20–60 minutes [10](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0250808).
    - **Histopathology:** Sections of skin and muscle from the leading edge of the lesion reveal hyphal invasion; periodic acid-Schiff (PAS) and Grocott's methenamine silver stains highlight the hyphae [5](https://doi.org/10.1111/jfd.12175).

**Differential Diagnosis**

- **Distinguishing Saprolegniasis from Other Conditions:** Other skin diseases of salmonids, such as columnaris disease (*Flavobacterium columnare*), *Aeromonas* infections and ciliate or monogenean parasites, also cause skin damage [7](https://doi.org/10.3390/microorganisms12081733). Related oomycetes such as *Aphanomyces* spp. can be excluded with species-specific qPCR targeting the ITS region of *S. parasitica* [8](https://doi.org/10.3390/microorganisms10112186).

### Treatment and Prevention

**Treatment Options**

- **Current Treatments:**
    - **Chemical Treatments:** Following the malachite green ban, registered treatments for saprolegniasis are scarce [12](https://doi.org/10.1111/jfd.12923). Bronopol (Pyceze®) is registered in some European countries for daily baths of eggs. Hydrogen peroxide is widely used as a replacement for malachite green, and one product is approved by the US Food and Drug Administration for hatcheries. Formalin is effective on eggs but is not approved as a veterinary medicine for live fish in most EU countries and is classified as a carcinogen. Sodium chloride baths improve hatching rates but need concentrations (20–30 g/L) that are hard to apply in intensive farms [12](https://doi.org/10.1111/jfd.12923) [18](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0031182022001093). Copper sulfate is widely used and inhibits *S. parasitica* by disrupting protein synthesis, energy biogenesis, and metabolism [12](https://doi.org/10.1111/jfd.12923) [14](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0147445).
    - **Historical Treatments:** Malachite green was used for decades as the gold-standard treatment against *Saprolegnia* on both fish and eggs. Its international ban in 2002 because of carcinogenic and teratogenic risks [13](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0091878) [21](https://doi.org/10.3389/fmicb.2019.02873) created a therapeutic gap that persists today, since no alternative with comparable efficacy has been found [12](https://doi.org/10.1111/jfd.12923).
    - **Treatment Challenges:** Approved alternatives are less effective than malachite green and vary by jurisdiction. Some carry environmental costs (bronopol is toxic to plankton and must be diluted before discharge), and relying repeatedly on a single control method favours the selection of tolerant pathogen strains [12](https://doi.org/10.1111/jfd.12923) [18](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0031182022001093).

- **Emerging Treatments:**
    - **Boric acid:** Inhibits *Saprolegnia* spore germination and mycelial growth in vitro, and effectively controls saprolegniasis in Atlantic salmon eyed eggs and yolk sac fry in vivo without adverse effects on hatchability or survival [13](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0091878).
    - **Linalool:** A natural terpene alcohol found in various essential oils, linalool exerts dual activity, directly disrupting *S. parasitica* cell membranes and inhibiting protein synthesis, while simultaneously stimulating host complement and coagulation cascades, wound healing, and immune cell responses [17](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.100393).
    - **Plant extracts:** Ethanolic extracts of pomegranate (*Punica granatum*) and thyme (*Thymus vulgaris*) significantly suppress mycelial growth of *S. diclina* at concentrations with low fish toxicity, representing promising natural alternatives to chemical fungicides [15](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.sjbs.2020.04.008).
    - **Chlorhexidine:** Identified by virtual screening against a cytochrome P450 fusion protein unique to *Saprolegnia*, chlorhexidine demonstrated strong anti-*Saprolegnia* activity in vitro (MIC₅₀ of 10.93 µg/ml), positioning it as a lead candidate for further in vivo development [16](https://doi.org/10.3389/fmicb.2025.1723326).
    - **Clotrimazole:** The imidazole antifungal arrested both spore and hyphal growth of *S. parasitica* isolated from catfish at a concentration of 2 mg/L, suggesting potential utility in non-salmonid aquaculture systems [6](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-024-80075-0).

**Preventive Measures**

- **Biosecurity Protocols:**
    - Regular egg disinfection with approved products (hydrogen peroxide, bronopol) throughout incubation [12](https://doi.org/10.1111/jfd.12923).
    - Prompt and thorough removal of dead and unfertilised eggs from incubation trays, since these are colonised first and the infection then spreads to healthy eggs [7](https://doi.org/10.3390/microorganisms12081733).
    - Disinfection of the entire production system between production cycles, and introduction of disinfected eggs from certified pathogen-free sources only [18](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0031182022001093).

- **Farm Management Practices:**
    - Minimising handling-induced injuries through careful grading, crowding, and transfer procedures to reduce entry sites for *Saprolegnia* [7](https://doi.org/10.3390/microorganisms12081733).
    - Maintaining good water quality and aeration, providing good nutrition, and avoiding crowding to minimise injuries [15](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.sjbs.2020.04.008).
    - Incorporating *Saprolegnia* resistance into selective breeding programs; heritability estimates of ~0.25 in Atlantic salmon indicate meaningful scope for genetic improvement [11](https://doi.org/10.1111/jfd.13664).

### Case Studies

**Real-World Examples**

- **Notable Outbreaks:**
    - **Scotland (Atlantic salmon hatcheries and farms):** A two-year surveillance study of 14 aquaculture sites in Scotland recovered 412 oomycete and fungal isolates from fish and water. *Saprolegnia* accounted for 66% of all oomycetes isolated, and *S. parasitica* phylotype S2 was overwhelmingly dominant, found at 12 of 14 sites and representing 91.1% of *S. parasitica* isolates during epizootic periods. The study demonstrated that water-column spore counts alone were insufficient to predict outbreak risk, highlighting the need for fish-derived sampling and molecular diagnostics [1](https://doi.org/10.3390/jof10010057).
    - **India (cage-cultured striped catfish):** *Saprolegnia parasitica* was isolated and characterized from cage-reared *Pangasianodon hypophthalmus* farmed in freshwater reservoirs in India. The pathogen displayed temperature-sensitive growth optimum of 12–24°C, consistent with heightened risk during cooler winter months. Clotrimazole was identified as an effective treatment candidate at 2 mg/L against both spore and hyphal stages [6](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-024-80075-0).
    - **Wild salmonids (ulcerative dermal necrosis, UDN):** UDN produces severe ulcerative skin lesions in sea trout and Atlantic salmon migrating from the sea into rivers to spawn; in Poland, 74.7% of sea trout caught in the Słupia River in 2007 showed clinical signs. Fish in the final stage are covered with ulcers and mould, but recent investigations point to a complex aetiology without a clearly defined pathogen, in which the change from salt water to fresh water alters skin physiology and increases susceptibility [19](https://doi.org/10.3390/pathogens13030251).

- **Lessons Learned:** Effective management requires integrated approaches combining molecular surveillance of both water and fish, early removal of infected material, stress minimization, and timely application of approved treatments. Single-intervention strategies are insufficient for reliable control [1](https://doi.org/10.3390/jof10010057) [18](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0031182022001093).

## Data Insights

### Disease Impact by Country

#### Scotland

- **Saprolegniasis Incidence:**
    - Saprolegniasis is among the most economically significant diseases affecting freshwater Atlantic salmon aquaculture in Scotland. A comprehensive two-year surveillance study across 14 farms demonstrated that *Saprolegnia* accounted for 66% of all oomycetes isolated [1](https://doi.org/10.3390/jof10010057).
    - Phylotype S2 of *S. parasitica* was the dominant strain at epizootic sites, detected at 12 of 14 sampling locations and representing 93.5% of fish-derived *S. parasitica* isolates and 91.1% of isolates recovered during outbreak periods [1](https://doi.org/10.3390/jof10010057).

- **Diagnostic Insights:**
    - Water sampling alone (spore counts) proved insufficient to predict outbreaks; S2 was isolated predominantly from fish rather than water samples, indicating that fish-derived diagnostics are essential for accurate surveillance and early warning [1](https://doi.org/10.3390/jof10010057).

#### Norway

- **Saprolegniasis Incidence:**
    - In Norway, problems associated with *Saprolegnia* are most pronounced in hatcheries. Saprolegniasis is usually diagnosed and treated in the field without laboratory confirmation, so the Norwegian Veterinary Institute records only a few cases each year and these do not reflect the true extent of the problem; in 2025, no cases were detected in submissions from farmed fish [22](https://www.vetinst.no/rapporter-og-publikasjoner/rapporter/2026/fish-health-report-2025/_/attachment/inline/4e7d310d-0620-439c-a2cf-caac02253620:b124880858959e1f6b61fbe45e1137e7e31fc0f9/Fish%20Health%20Report%202025.pdf).
    - In the 2025 annual fish health survey, 7 of 62 respondents reported that saprolegniasis had caused mortality in salmon in the freshwater phase, 5 of 60 reported negative effects on welfare, and 4 of 57 reported an increasing occurrence. Overall, the disease is perceived as a relatively minor problem that continues to be effectively controlled through preventive measures [22](https://www.vetinst.no/rapporter-og-publikasjoner/rapporter/2026/fish-health-report-2025/_/attachment/inline/4e7d310d-0620-439c-a2cf-caac02253620:b124880858959e1f6b61fbe45e1137e7e31fc0f9/Fish%20Health%20Report%202025.pdf).

- **Treatment & Management:**
    - Formalin is the most cost-effective agent and, in most cases, the first choice of treatment during an outbreak, although its use in aquaculture is controversial and remains under evaluation within the EU regulatory system. Prevention relies on gentle handling during grading, transfer and vaccination, good hygiene and water quality, and the frequent removal of dead eggs and residual organic material during incubation [22](https://www.vetinst.no/rapporter-og-publikasjoner/rapporter/2026/fish-health-report-2025/_/attachment/inline/4e7d310d-0620-439c-a2cf-caac02253620:b124880858959e1f6b61fbe45e1137e7e31fc0f9/Fish%20Health%20Report%202025.pdf).

#### Chile

- **Saprolegniasis Incidence:**
    - In Chile, saprolegniasis is not reported as a standalone disease. Freshwater mortality is recorded under the category **"Micosis"** (mycosis) of SERNAPESCA's mortality classification system (PSGM), which is declared by each hatchery and excludes eggs [20](https://www.sernapesca.cl/app/uploads/2026/03/Informe-Situacion-Sanitaria-Salmonicultura-Ano-2025.pdf).
    - In 2025, infectious causes accounted for 8.4% of all freshwater mortality in Atlantic salmon. Within that share, mycosis was the **leading infectious cause (60.2%)**, ahead of flavobacteriosis (24.9%), and increased by 7.5% compared with 2024, while mortality from IPN and flavobacteriosis decreased [20](https://www.sernapesca.cl/app/uploads/2026/03/Informe-Situacion-Sanitaria-Salmonicultura-Ano-2025.pdf).
    - In freshwater coho salmon, mycosis was the **second infectious cause of mortality** (34.4%), after flavobacteriosis (51.9%). It increased by 18% compared with 2024, while IPN decreased by 12.9% and flavobacteriosis by 3.1% [20](https://www.sernapesca.cl/app/uploads/2026/03/Informe-Situacion-Sanitaria-Salmonicultura-Ano-2025.pdf).
    - In rainbow trout, mycosis was a minor infectious cause (3.3%), with flavobacteriosis dominant (95.1%) [20](https://www.sernapesca.cl/app/uploads/2026/03/Informe-Situacion-Sanitaria-Salmonicultura-Ano-2025.pdf).

- **Production Context:**
    - Chile had a maximum of 140 active freshwater farms in 2025 (June), concentrated in Los Lagos (67), Araucanía (36), and Los Ríos (28) [20](https://www.sernapesca.cl/app/uploads/2026/03/Informe-Situacion-Sanitaria-Salmonicultura-Ano-2025.pdf).

#### India

- **Saprolegniasis Incidence:**
    - *Saprolegnia parasitica* has been confirmed as a significant pathogen of striped catfish (*Pangasianodon hypophthalmus*) in cage aquaculture systems in Indian freshwater reservoirs, representing a threat to one of South Asia's most economically important aquaculture species [6](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-024-80075-0).

- **Economic Impact:**
    - In the cages, 11.7% of the catfish died during the winter months, with prevalence, severity and mortality increasing as winter progressed; in experimental infections the pathogen caused high mortality in winter but no disease in summer [6](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-024-80075-0).

- **Treatment & Management:**
    - In vitro sensitivity testing identified clotrimazole (MIC: 2 mg/L) as effective against both spore and hyphal stages, while commonly available compounds (boric acid, fluconazole, sodium thiosulfate, and potassium permanganate) showed negligible activity against *S. parasitica* isolates from this system [6](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-024-80075-0).

## Conclusion

Saprolegniasis remains one of the most economically damaging infectious diseases in freshwater aquaculture worldwide, affecting salmonids, tilapia, catfish, and fish eggs. The withdrawal of malachite green from use in food fish production has left a persistent therapeutic gap: current approved alternatives (hydrogen peroxide, bronopol, formalin, and salt baths) provide partial control but are generally less effective, and the number of fully approved products varies substantially across jurisdictions [12](https://doi.org/10.1111/jfd.12923).

Effective management of saprolegniasis in modern aquaculture requires integrated strategies: rigorous biosecurity including regular egg disinfection, prompt removal of dead organic material, and minimization of handling stress; molecular surveillance tools capable of detecting and quantifying *S. parasitica* at the strain level in both fish and water [8](https://doi.org/10.3390/microorganisms10112186) [1](https://doi.org/10.3390/jof10010057); and exploitation of the heritable component of resistance in Atlantic salmon breeding programs [11](https://doi.org/10.1111/jfd.13664). Novel therapeutant candidates (linalool, chlorhexidine, boric acid, and plant-derived extracts) offer promising leads, but require robust in vivo validation and regulatory approval before widespread aquaculture use.

Future research priorities include: in vivo validation of emerging therapeutants across multiple host species; further characterization of *Saprolegnia* phylotype diversity and its relationship to virulence and geographic distribution; development of point-of-care molecular diagnostics for on-farm use; elucidation of host immune responses to guide immunostimulant strategies; and streamlined regulatory pathways to accelerate translation of research into farm practice.
