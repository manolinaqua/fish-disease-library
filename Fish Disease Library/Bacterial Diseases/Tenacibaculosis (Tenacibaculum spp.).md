## Overview

### What is Tenacibaculosis (*Tenacibaculum* spp.)?
Tenacibaculosis is a bacterial ulcerative disease caused by multiple species within the genus *Tenacibaculum*, affecting numerous fish species in marine aquaculture [1](https://doi.org/10.1128/AEM.01177-14). It is characterised by ulcerative and/or necrotic skin lesions, an eroded and haemorrhagic mouth, frayed fins, and tail rot. These clinical signs often lead to significant economic losses and are most commonly associated with high mortality in newly transferred smolt [2](https://www.vetinst.no/sykdom-og-agens/tenacibaculose), [3](https://doi.org/10.3389/fcimb.2022.1068000). The disease was first described in Japan in the 1970s and has since been reported in many marine wild and farmed fish in Europe, Asia, North and South America and Australia [3](https://doi.org/10.3389/fcimb.2022.1068000)[4](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0259215). Despite extensive research, there is still no complete treatment [2](https://www.vetinst.no/sykdom-og-agens/tenacibaculose), [3](https://doi.org/10.3389/fcimb.2022.1068000)[5](https://doi.org/10.3389/faquc.2024.1496943)

## Clinical Signs of Tenacibaculosis (*Tenacibaculum* spp.)

### Common Signs

- **Physical Signs:**
    - **Skin Ulcers:** Deep, necrotic ulcers on the skin often surrounded by a darkened area or zone of yellow. Ulcers often found around the mouth, head, fins, and body (for pictures, see Sandoval [6](https://fishhistopathology.com/?p=2369))
    - **Fin Erosion:** Fraying and degradation of the fins, especially the caudal (tail) fin [7](https://doi.org/10.3390/pathogens9121029)
    - **Haemorrhages:** Reddened areas on the skin and fins due to bleeding [7](https://doi.org/10.3390/pathogens9121029)
    - **Gill lesions:** erosion and necrosis with little inflammation [8](https://fishhistopathology.com/?p=2550)
    - **Systemic infections** may occur depending on the specific isolate, though *Tenacibaculum* is primarily characterized as a superficial infection targeting the skin, fins, and gills [8](https://fishhistopathology.com/?p=2550)
    - **Jawbone erosion**: In cases of *Tenacibaculum maritimum* yellow mats form on the inside of the mouth, especially in Atlantic salmon [9](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0206951). This often progresses to extensive necrosis and erosion of the jaw, which can lead to the loss of teeth in some cases [8](https://fishhistopathology.com/?p=2550) [10](https://akvademiet.no/fiskesykdommer/infeksjonssykdommer/tenacibaculose/).
    - **Eye infection**: Cloudy appearance in the eyes and exophthalmia (bulging) [11](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0187476). In severe cases, the infection can lead to necrosis of the eye [2](https://www.vetinst.no/sykdom-og-agens/tenacibaculose) [3](https://doi.org/10.3389/fcimb.2022.1068000).

- **Behavioural Changes:**
    - **Lethargy:** Reduced activity and slow swimming [10](https://akvademiet.no/fiskesykdommer/infeksjonssykdommer/tenacibaculose/).
    - **Loss of Appetite:** Decreased feeding behaviour and significant weight loss [12](https://web.archive.org/web/20251212000320/http://www.medaid-h2020.eu/index.php/2019/02/05/tenacibaculosis/) 
    - **Abnormal Swimming Patterns:** Fish may swim erratically, for example flashing [7](https://doi.org/10.3390/pathogens9121029)


**Progression of Clinical Signs**

- **Outbreak Course:** Typically, an outbreak within a single sea cage in Norway lasts approximately 14 days, characterized by one week of increasing mortality followed by a week of decline until the outbreak subsides [10](https://akvademiet.no/fiskesykdommer/infeksjonssykdommer/tenacibaculose/).
- **Impact on Fish Health:** Tenacibaculosis damages the skin barrier, which opens the way to secondary infections such as *Vibrio* spp. [13](https://doi.org/10.3354/dao071255).

### Causes of Tenacibaculosis (*Tenacibaculum* spp.)

**Etiology**
The genus *Tenacibaculum* currently contains a total of 40 valid fish-pathogenic and environmental species [14](https://lpsn.dsmz.de/genus/tenacibaculum). *Tenacibaculum* spp. are generally considered opportunistic pathogens, i.e. they do not normally cause disease, but can cause infection when the opportunity arises [3](https://doi.org/10.3389/fcimb.2022.1068000). Pathogenic species include: *T. maritimum*, *T. soleae*, *T. discolor*, *T. dicentrarchi*, *T. finnmarkense* and *T. ovolyticum* [15](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00253-018-9370-1). It was previously named flexibacteriosis. 


- **Causative Agents:** 
	- *T. maritimum* is one of the better researched species in the genus. It is also known as "yellow mouth disease" (in British Columbia, Canada and Washington State), “gliding bacterial diseases of sea fish”, "marine flexibacteriosis", “eroded mouth syndrome” and “black patch necrosis”. Originally described in 1970s with mortalities among farmed red (*Pagrus major*) and blackhead (*Acanthopagrus schlegelii*) seabream in Japan [3](https://doi.org/10.3389/fcimb.2022.1068000). *T. maritimum* isolates from Norway have also been associated with disease in lumpfish (*Cyclopterus lumpus*) under aquaculture conditions, where infections can present as characteristic crater-like lesions (“donut syndrome”) [16](https://www.fhf.no/prosjekter/prosjektbasen/901433/). It has strong adhesion to fish skin and mucus, optimum growth temperature in the laboratory is 30°C, gram negative and is thin and rod-shaped [3](https://doi.org/10.3389/fcimb.2022.1068000)
	- *T. soleae* identified from Senegalese sole (*Solea senegalensis*), and later from wedge sole, brill and European sea bass. Causes external ulcers [7](https://doi.org/10.3390/pathogens9121029)
	- *T. discolor* identified from a dead Senegalese sole (*Solea senegalensis*) in Spain [17](https://doi.org/10.1099/ijs.0.65397-0). It experimentally caused tenacibaculosis in turbot and sole, but it is not yet confirmed as a primary pathogen [7](https://doi.org/10.3390/pathogens9121029)
	- *T. dicentrarchi* identified from Atlantic salmon (*Salmo salar*) [4](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0259215) and European sea bass (*Dicentrarchus labrax* L.) [18](https://doi.org/10.1099/ijs.0.025122-0).
	- *T. finnmarkense* identified from Atlantic salmon (*Salmo salar*) in Norway [19](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10482-015-0630-0) and Chile [20](https://doi.org/10.1111/jfd.13213). Isolates closely related to *T. finnmarkense* (by 16S rRNA) caused the first outbreaks in rainbow trout (*Oncorhynchus mykiss*) and coho salmon, in Chile [20](https://doi.org/10.1111/jfd.13213). It is classified as a highly virulent pathogen in salmonids, characterized by its ability to cause significant tissue damage and high mortality even at low infectious doses, particularly during the vulnerable seawater transfer phase [2](https://www.vetinst.no/sykdom-og-agens/tenacibaculose). Experimental studies demonstrate temperature-dependent virulence where infection occurs more readily at low seawater temperatures (4 °C), while higher bacterial doses are required to establish infection at higher temperatures (8 °C) [16](https://www.fhf.no/prosjekter/prosjektbasen/901433/). The species is divided into two genetic sub-groups (called genomovars, gv): gv. *finnmarkense* and gv. *ulcerans* [21](https://www.fhf.no/prosjekter/prosjektbasen/901434/)
	- *T. ovolyticum* identified in Atlantic halibut (*Hippoglossus hippoglossus*) and farmed Atlantic salmon (*Salmo salar*) [22](https://doi.org/10.1128/mra.00254-22). Grows in cold seawater and associated with deep/cold water environments [23](https://doi.org/10.1128/genomeA.00644-16). Mainly an egg pathogen historically, but emerging as a cause of tenacibaculosis in farmed fish [4](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0259215).

- **Transmission Methods:**


    - **Direct Contact:** 
	    - *T. maritimum*, *T. dicentrarchi* can be horizontally transferred between fish [16](https://www.fhf.no/prosjekter/prosjektbasen/901433/), [7](https://doi.org/10.3390/pathogens9121029). 

    - **Waterborne:**
	    - *T. finnmarkense*: Different strains vary in their ability to cause disease, and experimental studies show variable and sometimes limited infection success, meaning transmission dynamics are not yet well understood [24](https://bora.uib.no/bora-xmlui/handle/1956/19440).

**Risk Factors**
Disease from *T. maritimum* peaks in a 15–26°C range but varies by location/fish species; stress, age/size and bad conditions which make outbreaks worse [3](https://doi.org/10.3389/fcimb.2022.1068000).

- **Host-Specific Factors:** Younger and smaller Atlantic salmon are significantly more susceptible to acute infection and severe clinical signs [5](https://doi.org/10.3389/faquc.2024.1496943). 
     - Susceptibility is highest during the first year of production at sea [5](https://doi.org/10.3389/faquc.2024.1496943).



- **Environmental Factors:** Salinity, water temperature, seasonality, algal or jellyfish blooms can affect susceptibility to Tenacibaculosis [5](https://doi.org/10.3389/faquc.2024.1496943), [3](https://doi.org/10.3389/fcimb.2022.1068000). 
	- Some regions: more cases with warming waters (Tasmania); others: winter outbreaks (15–20°C ideal in Spain). [3](https://doi.org/10.3389/fcimb.2022.1068000)
	- For *T. finnmarkense* experimental studies demonstrate temperature-dependent virulence where infection occurs more readily at low seawater temperatures (4 °C), while higher bacterial doses are required to establish infection at higher temperatures (8 °C) [16](https://www.fhf.no/prosjekter/prosjektbasen/901433/). Consequently, colder seawater conditions (<8 °C) are associated with an increased risk of disease [10](https://akvademiet.no/fiskesykdommer/infeksjonssykdommer/tenacibaculose/).


- **Farm Management Practices:** Generally high rearing density, excessive feed administration (especially in the case of *T. maritimum* which focuses on the mouth [8](https://fishhistopathology.com/?p=2550)) , fresh to saltwater transfer of smolts (salmonids) and/or mechanical damage of the skin and mucus barrier, and healthy fish carrying the bacteria [3](https://doi.org/10.3389/fcimb.2022.1068000), [26](https://doi.org/10.3354/dao02329), [5](https://doi.org/10.3389/faquc.2024.1496943), [3](https://doi.org/10.3389/fcimb.2022.1068000). Some specific risks for other species:
	- For *T. finnmarkense* transferring salmonid smolts to seawater during periods of low temperature increases risk of tenacibaculosis infection. Consequently, the disease is most prevalent in Norway during the early spring and late autumn transfer windows, when water temperatures are at their lowest [16](https://www.fhf.no/prosjekter/prosjektbasen/901433/) [10](https://akvademiet.no/fiskesykdommer/infeksjonssykdommer/tenacibaculose/).
	- For *T. dicentrarchi* skin or gill damage increases disease susceptibility [7](https://doi.org/10.3390/pathogens9121029). 
	- For *T. ovolyticum*, mortality of Atlantic halibut eggs at hatching rises sharply when it makes up more than 30% of the bacteria on the egg surface [7](https://doi.org/10.3390/pathogens9121029).
	- For *T. soleae*, it is primarily associated with marine aquaculture of flatfish species such as sole, turbot, and brill, particularly in southern Europe (e.g., Spain), where it has caused significant disease outbreaks and economic losses [27](https://doi.org/10.3147/jsfp.49.16).

- **Co-infection:** In Chile (2018–2021), piscirickettsiosis (SRS), sea lice (caligidosis) and mechanical injuries were each associated with reporting tenacibaculosis, but after adjusting for other factors only infestation with *Caligus rogercresseyi* remained significant (odds ratio 3.23) [28](https://doi.org/10.1111/jfd.70249).
	- In a microbiome study of Atlantic salmon infected with *Piscirickettsia salmonis*, skin ulcers were colonised mainly by *P. salmonis*, *T. dicentrarchi* and *Aliivibrio wodanis*. The authors suggest that co-infection may increase the pathogenicity of *P. salmonis*, but this has not been tested [29](https://doi.org/10.3390/ani14010097).

### Diagnosis

**Diagnostic Methods**
Diagnostic methods for *Tenacibaculum* infections primarily involve culture isolation, microscopy, molecular techniques like PCR and MLSA, and proteomics such as MALDI-TOF MS [7](https://doi.org/10.3390/pathogens9121029)

- **Clinical Examination:** Observation of clinical signs, such as skin ulcers and fin erosion.

- **Laboratory Tests:**
    - **Bacterial Culture & microscopy**: Isolation and identification of *Tenacibaculum* spp. from skin or lesion samples on marine agar media. Colonies are typically yellow-pigmented, and microscopy reveals long, filamentous rod-shaped bacteria characteristic of the genus [2](https://www.vetinst.no/sykdom-og-agens/tenacibaculose). Species identification can be confirmed using MALDI-TOF MS or biochemical tests [7](https://doi.org/10.3390/pathogens9121029).
    - **PCR (Polymerase Chain Reaction):** PCR can be performed directly from tissue lesions or from cultured isolates. Detects bacterial DNA. Species-specific PCR assays have been developed for *T. dicentrarchi*, *T. maritimum*, *T. finnmarkense* and *T. soleae* [30](https://doi.org/10.3354/dao03407) [31](https://doi.org/10.1111/jfd.13357) qPCR is widely used in diagnostic laboratories for surveillance and estimation of bacterial load, while methods such as multilocus sequence analysis (MLSA) are primarily applied in research settings to assess strain diversity [32](https://ressources.ciheam.org/ressources/om/pdf/b75/00007944.pdf), [4](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0259215) 
    - **Histopathology:** Microscopic examination of tissue samples to assess the extent of tissue damage and infection where you can see presence of filamentous bacteria, often forming dense mats along the lesion margins. Does not allow species-level ID [3](https://doi.org/10.3389/fcimb.2022.1068000)[13](https://doi.org/10.3354/dao071255)


**Differential Diagnosis**

- **Distinguishing Tenacibaculosis from Other Diseases:** *Tenacibaculum* spp. can often be in the wounds with other bacteria such as [[Moritella Viscosa|*Moritella viscosa*]], *Aliivibrio wodanis* and different *Vibrio* species.[33](https://www.vetinst.no/sykdom-og-agens/vintersar)[34](https://nofima.no/fakta/verdt-a-vite-om-bakterielle-sar-pa-laks/) Laboratory confirmation (e.g., PCR or culture) is required for differentiation.
- Clinically, *Tenacibaculum* spp. infections in salmonids are typically characterized by primarily surface-associated erosive lesions affecting the skin, fins, and mouth, sometimes with a yellowish appearance due to bacterial growth. In contrast, infections caused by *Moritella viscosa* more often present as deep ulcerative lesions and may involve systemic infection [35](https://www.vetinst.no/rapporter-og-publikasjoner/rapporter/2026/fiskehelserapporten-2025).
- Infections are often with multiple strains or even *Tenacibaculum* species, which also results in poor treatment efficacy [12](https://web.archive.org/web/20251212000320/http://www.medaid-h2020.eu/index.php/2019/02/05/tenacibaculosis/) [4](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0259215).

### Treatment and Prevention

**Treatment Options**
A summary table for each species is found below.
- **Current Treatments:**
    - **Antibiotics:** Used in some cases to control *T. maritimum* with varying degrees of success [3](https://doi.org/10.3389/fcimb.2022.1068000) Administered through medicated feed or water to treat bacterial infections. The bacteria are naturally resistant to quinolones (a type of antibiotics commonly used against gram-negative bacteria), so efficacy is limited [2](https://www.vetinst.no/sykdom-og-agens/tenacibaculose)


- **Supportive Care:** 
    - **Improving Water Quality:** Ensuring optimal water conditions to support wound healing and reduce stress [36](https://doi.org/10.3390/ani15172581)
    - **Reducing Stocking Densities:** Reduce stocking density during the whole life cycle to keep stress levels down (low stress = good immunity), reduce transmission pressure and minimise skin damage [36](https://doi.org/10.3390/ani15172581)
    - **Nutritional support:** Especially during first feeding, use high quality diets, and functional ingredients (e.g., immunostimulants, certain algae, probiotics or prebiotics) that may enhance skin and gut barriers and reduce lesion severity [36](https://doi.org/10.3390/ani15172581). Essential oils have also shown to reduce mortalities in experimental studies with sea bream [37](https://doi.org/10.20935/AcadBiol7847).

**Preventive Measures**
In Norway, antibiotics are rarely used against tenacibaculosis because of their limited effect, so it is managed mainly through prevention [2](https://www.vetinst.no/sykdom-og-agens/tenacibaculose).

- **Biosecurity Protocols:**
    - Regular monitoring of fish health to detect and address (either by antibiotics or culling) early signs of infection [3](https://doi.org/10.3389/fcimb.2022.1068000) 
    - Genotyping shows no sign that *T. maritimum* strains have spread long distances through international fish movements; strains appear to be endemic to each area, which helps with tracking outbreaks [1](https://doi.org/10.1128/AEM.01177-14).

    
- **Vaccines**:
	- No commercial vaccine, except for one for turbot in Spain, which needs a booster dose [3](https://doi.org/10.3389/fcimb.2022.1068000). In Chile, no vaccine is currently available against *T. dicentrarchi* [40](https://doi.org/10.1111/jfd.70013). Antigenic diversity between strains makes it difficult to choose strains for new vaccines [3](https://doi.org/10.3389/fcimb.2022.1068000) 
	- Tested vaccines include: barramundi in Singapore, salmon in Tasmania, salmon in Chile [12](https://web.archive.org/web/20251212000320/http://www.medaid-h2020.eu/index.php/2019/02/05/tenacibaculosis/), whole cell inactivated adjuvanted vaccine against *T. finnmarkense* [24](https://bora.uib.no/bora-xmlui/handle/1956/19440) and immunization with recombinant proteins against *T. finnmarkense* [42](https://www.fhf.no/prosjekter/prosjektbasen/901655/)


- **Farm Management Practices:**
    - Minimising handling, crowding, and mechanical stress (e.g. during transport and delousing) reduces skin damage and infection entry points, especially when temperatures are below 8°C [10](https://akvademiet.no/fiskesykdommer/infeksjonssykdommer/tenacibaculose/). 
    - Controlling stocking density (avoiding overcrowding), reducing stress and avoiding overfeeding can prevent outbreaks or reduce their incidence [13](https://doi.org/10.3354/dao071255)
    - Salmonids that have been exposed to low-strength salinity appear to have a better ability to respond to an infection. [16](https://www.fhf.no/prosjekter/prosjektbasen/901433/)
    - Seasonal awareness - Handling operations are often adjusted based on seasonal and temperature-related risk periods to reduce disease susceptibility. Transferring salmonids to seawater at temperatures above 5 °C has been shown to significantly reduce infection risk from *T. finnmarkense* [21](https://www.fhf.no/prosjekter/prosjektbasen/901434/)

### Case Studies

**Real-World Examples**

- **Notable Outbreaks:**
    - **Japan (1970s):** Early outbreaks of what is now known as *T. maritimum* (formerly *Flexibacter maritimus*) were reported in cultured marine fish, including red seabream (*Pagrus major*) and black seabream (*Acanthopagrus schlegelii*), causing significant mortality and leading to the initial description of the pathogen [3](https://doi.org/10.3389/fcimb.2022.1068000). 
    
    - **Norway (2010s):** Several high mortality outbreaks of skin lesions/ulcers in sea-reared Atlantic salmon occurred in all regions along the Norwegian coast at low seawater temperatures, although the bacteria had been present since at least the 1980s [24](https://bora.uib.no/bora-xmlui/handle/1956/19440) [4](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0259215). This coincides with changes in farming practices (more handling operations) and expansion into colder regions.
	    - In northern Norway, especially Finnmark, species such as *T. finnmarkense* have been linked to significant outbreaks. In September 2015, outbreaks at two farms in Finnmark caused up to 40% cumulative mortality in net-pens of recently transferred smolts [11](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0187476).
    
    - **Chile (since 2018):** Tenacibaculosis became an important problem in sea-farmed salmonids, with *T. dicentrarchi* as the main species. In 2018 it was added to Chile's list of high-risk diseases [43](https://infosalmon.cl/adl-diagnostic-chile-entrega-conocimiento-clave-sobre-tenacibaculosis-en-la-salmonicultura/).
	    - **2020 new hosts**: The first clinical tenacibaculosis outbreaks in farmed rainbow trout and coho salmon worldwide were documented at five marine farms in the Los Lagos, Aysén and Magallanes Regions. Most isolates were more closely related to *T. finnmarkense* than to *T. dicentrarchi* [20](https://doi.org/10.1111/jfd.13213).

    - **Europe:** The first European cases affected European sea bass (*Dicentrarchus labrax*) farms on the French Mediterranean coast; later cases were diagnosed in Italy, Greece and Turkey [44](https://doi.org/10.3389/fimmu.2023.1254677).
        - **2009–2010 Spain**: Three outbreaks in wedge sole at 20.5°C; *T. maritimum* isolated from ulcers [3](https://doi.org/10.3389/fcimb.2022.1068000) 

- **Lessons Learned:** Outbreaks are driven mainly by environmental conditions and stressors rather than by moving infected stock, so prevention relies on good husbandry: avoiding overcrowding, reducing stress and handling damage, and early diagnosis [13](https://doi.org/10.3354/dao071255)[45](https://www.mpi.govt.nz/dmsdocument/64254/direct).

#### Canada

- **Tenacibaculosis Incidence in Canada:**
    - Mouthrot, caused by *T. maritimum*, is a significant issue for farmed Atlantic salmon in British Columbia, and its treatment is the industry's greatest use of antimicrobials [46](https://waves-vagues.dfo-mpo.gc.ca/library-bibliotheque/40952824.pdf)
    - Diagnosed every year since 2003 in BC, with 106 farm-level diagnoses in fish health audits from 2002 to 2018 [46](https://waves-vagues.dfo-mpo.gc.ca/library-bibliotheque/40952824.pdf)
    - In Atlantic Canada, tenacibaculosis in Atlantic salmon is caused by *T. finnmarkense*, with clinical signs similar to those described in Norway [46](https://waves-vagues.dfo-mpo.gc.ca/library-bibliotheque/40952824.pdf)

- **Geographical Spread:**
    - Mouthrot has been diagnosed in all BC Fish Health Surveillance Zones where audits took place, and also in farmed Chinook salmon; it has not been diagnosed in farmed coho salmon in BC [46](https://waves-vagues.dfo-mpo.gc.ca/library-bibliotheque/40952824.pdf)
    - Tenacibaculosis has also been described in wild-caught Chinook salmon held in captivity in BC [47](https://doi.org/10.1101/2023.02.17.529034)

- **Economic Impact:**
    - Mouthrot typically occurs after smolts are transferred to sea and can cause up to 40% cumulative mortality in affected cages. In BC, the annual cost of outbreaks is estimated at $1.8 million, plus an estimated revenue loss of $3.8 million per year [38](https://www.cermaq.com/news/benchmark-animal-health-and-cermaq-receive-funding-from-the-research-council-of-norway-for-the-development-of-tenacibaculum-vaccines-for-salmon)

- **Treatment & Management:**
    - Florfenicol and potentiated sulfonamides are typically used to treat mouthrot [46](https://waves-vagues.dfo-mpo.gc.ca/library-bibliotheque/40952824.pdf)
    - Research efforts focus on developing vaccines to prevent the disease [38](https://www.cermaq.com/news/benchmark-animal-health-and-cermaq-receive-funding-from-the-research-council-of-norway-for-the-development-of-tenacibaculum-vaccines-for-salmon), [48](https://aquatechcenter.com/disease_challenge/mouth-rot/)
    - In-feed immune boosting agents have been suggested, although none have been tested [46](https://waves-vagues.dfo-mpo.gc.ca/library-bibliotheque/40952824.pdf)

#### Chile

- **Tenacibaculosis Incidence in Chile:**
    - Tenacibaculosis has been on Chile's list of high-risk diseases (Lista 3, Enfermedades de Alto Riesgo) since 2018, so diagnoses in farms are under official surveillance [43](https://infosalmon.cl/adl-diagnostic-chile-entrega-conocimiento-clave-sobre-tenacibaculosis-en-la-salmonicultura/).
    - *T. dicentrarchi* is the most virulent species and the one most often involved in outbreaks, while *T. maritimum* and *T. finnmarkense* play a minor role [43](https://infosalmon.cl/adl-diagnostic-chile-entrega-conocimiento-clave-sobre-tenacibaculosis-en-la-salmonicultura/). *T. ovolyticum*, *T. piscium* and *T. bernardetii* have also been reported in Chile [49](https://doi.org/10.1099/ijsem.0.006963).
    - In 2025, tenacibaculosis was the second leading infectious cause of mortality in sea-farmed Atlantic salmon after SRS, accounting for 31.5% of infectious mortality. Its share rose compared with 2024 in Atlantic salmon (2.1%), rainbow trout (6.8%) and coho salmon (4.0%) [50](https://www.sernapesca.cl/app/uploads/2026/03/Informe-Situacion-Sanitaria-Salmonicultura-Ano-2025.pdf).
    - *T. dicentrarchi* was the second most frequently detected pathogen in seawater farms in 2025, after piscine orthoreovirus (PRV) [50](https://www.sernapesca.cl/app/uploads/2026/03/Informe-Situacion-Sanitaria-Salmonicultura-Ano-2025.pdf).
    - Outbreaks usually appear at two points in the sea phase: after seawater transfer and again once fish pass 1 kg. Predisposing factors include handling and transport stress, smoltification, changes in salinity and temperature, algal blooms, low oxygen and co-infection with *Piscirickettsia salmonis* [43](https://infosalmon.cl/adl-diagnostic-chile-entrega-conocimiento-clave-sobre-tenacibaculosis-en-la-salmonicultura/).
    - A new species, *T. salmonis*, was isolated in 2018 from the gills of a diseased Atlantic salmon during a tenacibaculosis outbreak, but in an immersion challenge it did not cause significant mortality on its own [49](https://doi.org/10.1099/ijsem.0.006963).

- **Geographical Spread:**
    - *Tenacibaculum* species have been isolated from marine salmon farms across Chilean Patagonia, from the Los Lagos Region to the Magallanes Region, almost 1,200 km from north to south [49](https://doi.org/10.1099/ijsem.0.006963).

- **Economic Impact:**
    - Chile's salmon industry is the most affected by infectious diseases among the top producing countries, with estimated revenue losses of over US$700 million a year [51](https://doi.org/10.3390/antibiotics14121177). Tenacibaculosis is the second leading infectious cause of mortality in sea-farmed Atlantic salmon, after SRS [50](https://www.sernapesca.cl/app/uploads/2026/03/Informe-Situacion-Sanitaria-Salmonicultura-Ano-2025.pdf).

- **Treatment & Management:**
    - Chilean salmon farming uses large amounts of antibiotics compared with other producing countries, which raises concern about antimicrobial resistance [51](https://doi.org/10.3390/antibiotics14121177). Most of it targets SRS: in production cycles closed in 2025, 97.5% of antimicrobial use was for piscirickettsiosis, with smaller amounts for BKD and tenacibaculosis [52](https://www.sernapesca.cl/app/uploads/2026/03/Informe-de-uso-de-antimicrobianos-ano-2025.pdf).
    - Florfenicol is the drug of choice for all bacterial diseases except tenacibaculosis, where tiamulin accounted for more than 24% of the antimicrobials used in 2025 [52](https://www.sernapesca.cl/app/uploads/2026/03/Informe-de-uso-de-antimicrobianos-ano-2025.pdf).
    - No vaccine is currently available against *T. dicentrarchi* [40](https://doi.org/10.1111/jfd.70013). Research and industry efforts focus on vaccines, probiotics and regulatory measures such as density limits [51](https://doi.org/10.3390/antibiotics14121177).
    - Programs to reduce antibiotic use: the [Pincoy project](https://proyectopincoy.com/en/), for example, aims to reduce antibiotic use in Chilean salmon aquaculture.

#### Norway

- **Tenacibaculosis Incidence in Norway:**
    - Tenacibaculosis has been present in Norway since the late 1980s and leads to regular ulcerative outbreaks and high mortalities in farmed salmonids, particularly following seawater transfer. [24](https://bora.uib.no/bora-xmlui/handle/1956/19440)[4](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0259215)
    - Significant outbreaks have been reported, with *Tenacibaculum finnmarkense* being the most commonly isolated bacterium during these events, *T. dicentrarchi* noted in some post-smolt events [24](https://bora.uib.no/bora-xmlui/handle/1956/19440). 
    - A study from 2017-2020 confirmed the high diversity of *Tenacibaculum* strains (66) in Norway, with 95% of the strains isolated being previously unidentified [4](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0259215).
    - Norwegian *T. maritimum* isolates have been associated with “crater disease” (“donut syndrome”) in lumpfish (*Cyclopterus lumpus*), a condition characterized by circular erosive skin lesions. It has been reported to be directly transmitted (horizontally) to salmon [16](https://www.fhf.no/prosjekter/prosjektbasen/901433/)
    - In 2025, winter ulcers of any cause were recorded at 299 salmon farming sites in Norway. *Tenacibaculum* spp. were detected at 147 sites (165 in 2024), and 94 of these also had *Moritella viscosa*, so mixed infections are common [35](https://www.vetinst.no/rapporter-og-publikasjoner/rapporter/2026/fiskehelserapporten-2025).
    - The Tff-LDC variant of *T. finnmarkense* gv. *finnmarkense* continues to dominate among Norwegian salmon with tenacibaculosis [53](https://www.vetinst.no/rapporter-og-publikasjoner/rapporter/2026/sarbakterier-i-sjobasert-lakseoppdrett).
    - Official statistics likely underreport the true prevalence. Because tenacibaculosis and *M. viscosa* are not notifiable diseases and are easily diagnosed in the field, many cases are managed on-site without laboratory confirmation [35](https://www.vetinst.no/rapporter-og-publikasjoner/rapporter/2026/fiskehelserapporten-2025).

- **Geographical Spread:**
    - *Tenacibaculum* spp. have been detected along the entire Norwegian coast, from Akershus in the southeast to Finnmark in the north [4](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0259215). The north is most affected by Tenacibaculosis outbreaks, largely due to the colder water temperatures [4](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0259215). In 2025, however, southern and central Norway appeared more affected than in previous years, when cases were spread fairly evenly along the coast [35](https://www.vetinst.no/rapporter-og-publikasjoner/rapporter/2026/fiskehelserapporten-2025).
    - Seawater monitoring at farms in production areas 3, 6 and 12 (2023 to 2026) showed that infection pressure from *T. finnmarkense* in the water rises at low sea temperatures, sometimes a few weeks before ulcer-related mortality increases. This suggests that qPCR screening of seawater could help farmers plan stressful operations [53](https://www.vetinst.no/rapporter-og-publikasjoner/rapporter/2026/sarbakterier-i-sjobasert-lakseoppdrett).
    - Several strains, such as *Tenacibaculum dicentrarchi*, are present within restricted areas, while others are found across different localities and hosts [16](https://www.fhf.no/prosjekter/prosjektbasen/901433/)

- **Economic Impact:**
    - Tenacibaculosis in 2020 was considered the fifth most important cause of mortality in on-growing salmon in Norway [38](https://www.cermaq.com/news/benchmark-animal-health-and-cermaq-receive-funding-from-the-research-council-of-norway-for-the-development-of-tenacibaculum-vaccines-for-salmon).
    - In the 2025 national health survey, *Tenacibaculum* infections were generally rated as a minor problem and ranked among the ten most important health problems only in production areas 10 to 13, in northern Norway [35](https://www.vetinst.no/rapporter-og-publikasjoner/rapporter/2026/fiskehelserapporten-2025).
    - The disease causes major economic losses due to increased mortality, reduced quality at harvest, and the need for antibiotic treatments [38](https://www.cermaq.com/news/benchmark-animal-health-and-cermaq-receive-funding-from-the-research-council-of-norway-for-the-development-of-tenacibaculum-vaccines-for-salmon).

- **Treatment & Management:**
    - There are no commercially available vaccines yet, but recent efforts include the development of effective vaccines against *Tenacibaculum* bacteria [38](https://www.cermaq.com/news/benchmark-animal-health-and-cermaq-receive-funding-from-the-research-council-of-norway-for-the-development-of-tenacibaculum-vaccines-for-salmon). 
    - An experimental study shows that keeping smolt for 4 weeks in 26 per mille seawater can significantly reduce the susceptibility to tenacibaculosis after transfer to the sea and they can better respond to an infection [16](https://www.fhf.no/prosjekter/prosjektbasen/901433/).
    - Gentle handling during smolt transport and minimizing mechanical stress (e.g., during pumping or delousing) is critical as skin damage serves as the primary entry point for the bacteria. Especially when the sea temperature is < 8°C [10](https://akvademiet.no/fiskesykdommer/infeksjonssykdommer/tenacibaculose/).

#### New Zealand

- **Tenacibaculosis Incidence in New Zealand:**
	- *T. maritimum* was first implicated in disease in farmed Chinook salmon (*Oncorhynchus tshawytscha*) in 2015, during investigation of summer mortality events at New Zealand King Salmon farms in the Marlborough Sounds. It was isolated from fish with skin ulcers and from apparently healthy fish [45](https://www.mpi.govt.nz/dmsdocument/64254/direct)
	- Experimental challenges showed that both *T. maritimum* and *T. dicentrarchi* cause disease in Chinook salmon [25](https://doi.org/10.1101/2024.03.06.583827)

- **Geographical Spread:**
	- *T. maritimum* is considered widespread in New Zealand's marine environment. It was detected in the Marlborough Sounds, Akaroa Harbour (Canterbury) and Big Glory Bay (Stewart Island) [45](https://www.mpi.govt.nz/dmsdocument/64254/direct)

- **Economic Impact:**
	- Since the summer mortality events began in 2012, yearly cumulative mortality at New Zealand King Salmon farms has risen from about 25% to 30%. These events are multifactorial, with New Zealand rickettsia-like organism (NZ-RLO) and high water temperatures involved, and there is no evidence that *T. maritimum* is a primary pathogen [45](https://www.mpi.govt.nz/dmsdocument/64254/direct)

- **Treatment & Management:**
	- Since late 2022, New Zealand King Salmon vaccinates all fish going to sea with a bivalent autogenous vaccine against NZ-RLO1 and *T. maritimum* [45](https://www.mpi.govt.nz/dmsdocument/64254/direct)
	- Other practices include moving operations to cooler-water sites, reducing stocking densities and handling stress, and fallowing sites for at least 30 days [45](https://www.mpi.govt.nz/dmsdocument/64254/direct)
	- MPI movement controls in the Marlborough Sounds (in place since 2016) target NZ-RLO; no biosecurity measures are specifically in place for *T. maritimum* [45](https://www.mpi.govt.nz/dmsdocument/64254/direct)

#### Europe
- **Tenacibaculosis Incidence in Europe:**
	- In the Mediterranean it affects European sea bass (*Dicentrarchus labrax*), gilthead sea bream (*Sparus aurata*) and flatfish such as sole and turbot. Together with vibriosis, it is considered one of the most important diseases of sea bass, with mortalities over 30% in some marine areas and recirculation systems [12](https://web.archive.org/web/20251212000320/http://www.medaid-h2020.eu/index.php/2019/02/05/tenacibaculosis/)
	- Outbreaks mainly affect fish from the nursery phase to pre-growing (up to 80 to 100 g), and prevalence and severity increase above 15°C [12](https://web.archive.org/web/20251212000320/http://www.medaid-h2020.eu/index.php/2019/02/05/tenacibaculosis/)
	- In France, *T. maritimum* accounted for only about half of the *Tenacibaculum* strains isolated from clinical cases [12](https://web.archive.org/web/20251212000320/http://www.medaid-h2020.eu/index.php/2019/02/05/tenacibaculosis/)

- **Türkiye:**
	- In a 2020 to 2025 surveillance of 748 cage-level disease outbreaks in sea bass at 19 farms on the Aegean coast, *T. maritimum* was the second most common bacterial cause (35.8% of outbreaks), after *Photobacterium damselae* subsp. *piscicida* (48.8%) [54](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0362366)
	- It was isolated from fish of all sizes (2.5 to 2,500 g); in larger fish, cases were linked to handling operations that caused mechanical damage [54](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0362366)

- **Treatment & Management:**
	- Antibiotics in medicated feed or as bath treatments, which raises concerns on resistance [3](https://doi.org/10.3389/fcimb.2022.1068000) 
	- Tenacibaculosis is rated as a high-impact disease in Mediterranean sea bass farming because it is difficult to treat [55](https://backend.orbit.dtu.dk/ws/files/140640404/801957.Zrncic_workshop_report.pdf), and field results often do not match laboratory antibiotic susceptibility tests [12](https://web.archive.org/web/20251212000320/http://www.medaid-h2020.eu/index.php/2019/02/05/tenacibaculosis/)

## Conclusion

Tenacibaculosis remains a significant and increasing threat to finfish aquaculture worldwide (with some regional variation), with a diversity of species and strains affecting a diversity of fish. Effective treatment options are difficult to develop, though there is increased research and focus on better support for the fish earlier in life in addition to focus on prevention rather than treatments that include antibiotics. Some vaccines show promise in experimental studies, but it is still too early to say. 
By implementing robust biosecurity protocols, investing in ongoing research, and fostering industry-wide cooperation, some of the impact of Tenacibaculosis can be mitigated and ensure the sustainability of aquaculture operations. This comprehensive guide aims to provide the necessary information and resources to help manage Tenacibaculosis and support the health and welfare of farmed fish.
