## Overview

### What is Bacterial Cold-Water Disease (*Flavobacterium psychrophilum*)?

Bacterial Cold-Water Disease (BCWD, the term generally used in North America), also known as Rainbow Trout Fry Syndrome (RTFS) in Europe, is caused by *Flavobacterium psychrophilum*, a Gram-negative, non-flagellated bacterium (family Flavobacteriaceae, phylum Bacteroidota) that is either non-motile or moves by gliding rather than via flagella [1](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jare.2014.10.009). It is one of the three species recognized as the "big three" fish-pathogenic flavobacteria, alongside *F. columnare* (columnaris disease) and *F. branchiophilum* (bacterial gill disease) [1](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jare.2014.10.009).

The disease has other names. Low-temperature disease was an early North American term, and the bacterium was previously named *Cytophaga psychrophila* and *Flexibacter psychrophilus* [1](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jare.2014.10.009). Peduncle disease refers to the ulcers that spread over the caudal peduncle [2](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0039126). Flavobacteriosis is the name used in Chilean and Norwegian (*flavobakteriose*) surveillance reports [3](https://www.sernapesca.cl/app/uploads/2026/03/Informe-Situacion-Sanitaria-Salmonicultura-Ano-2025.pdf) [4](https://www.vetinst.no/rapporter-og-publikasjoner/rapporter/2026/fiskehelserapporten-2025).

*F. psychrophilum* is a psychrophilic pathogen of cold-water salmonid hatcheries, principally rainbow trout (*Oncorhynchus mykiss*), Atlantic salmon (*Salmo salar*), and coho salmon (*O. kisutch*). It causes skin/gill lesions and systemic disease with reported mortality of 30–90% in juvenile fish, and has been reported from Europe, North America, Chile, Japan, Korea, and Australia [5](https://doi.org/10.1111/jfd.14119) [6](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13567-017-0448-z). Its host range is wider than salmonids: in China it has been isolated in recent years from farmed cyprinids (crucian carp, grass carp, and silver carp) and identified as a causal agent of overwintering mortality syndrome [7](https://doi.org/10.3390/ani16101465) [8](https://doi.org/10.3389/fmicb.2026.1751566).

## Clinical Signs of Bacterial Cold-Water Disease (*Flavobacterium psychrophilum*)

### Common Signs

- **Physical Signs:**
    - RTFS episodes tend to occur at 10–14 °C [6](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13567-017-0448-z), although outbreaks also occur at much lower temperatures. The first Norwegian Atlantic salmon case, for example, occurred below 5 °C [9](https://doi.org/10.3354/dao02390). Necrotic lesions are often seen on the skin surrounding the dorsal fin and tail. Affected fish show haemorrhage (mouth, dorsal fin, operculum, pelvic fin, trunk), splenomegaly with blurred spleen margins, trunk lesions, and an eroded tail and mouth [6](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13567-017-0448-z).
    - Affected fish often show increased dark pigmentation, particularly towards the tail ("black tail") [10](https://doi.org/10.2174/1874401X01104010040).
    - Ulcerative lesions typically start around the adipose fin and progressively extend over the whole caudal peduncle [2](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0039126). In severe cases the ulcer leaves the vertebral column fully exposed [1](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jare.2014.10.009).
    - Other reported signs include lower jaw ulcers, pale or necrotic gills, increased mucus production, exophthalmia, anaemia, ascites, and a pale liver and kidney [10](https://doi.org/10.2174/1874401X01104010040).
    - In coho salmon reaching the grow-out phase in estuarine water, clinical signs are similar to those of the freshwater phase. Affected fish show descaling, erosions, and single or multiple ulcers with yellow pigmentation and white or haemorrhagic borders. The yellow colour comes from flexirubin, a pigment characteristic of the genus *Flavobacterium* [11](https://marcosgodoy.com/flavobacteriosis-en-salmon-coho-oncorhynchus-kisutch-en-estuario-patologia-macroscopica/).
- **Behavioural Signs:** Fish are lethargic, and nervous disorders such as spiral swimming have been reported [10](https://doi.org/10.2174/1874401X01104010040).

**Progression of Clinical Signs**

- **Early Stages:** One of the first signs is whitish material along a fin margin, followed by progressive necrosis [10](https://doi.org/10.2174/1874401X01104010040).
- **Advanced Stages:** Larger fish are more likely to develop the deep necrotic lesions of classic BCWD [10](https://doi.org/10.2174/1874401X01104010040). In rainbow trout fry, the disease mostly takes a septicaemic form (RTFS) with high mortality, and specific clinical signs may be absent or limited to ulcers with severe enlargement of the spleen [2](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0039126).
- **Chronic Cases:** Neurological signs such as spiral swimming and spinal deformities can persist long after an infection [10](https://doi.org/10.2174/1874401X01104010040). In an experimental study, rainbow trout infected as fry had more, and more severe, vertebral column deformities nine months later than uninfected controls, and these deformities were often visible externally [12](https://doi.org/10.1046/j.1365-2109.2001.00552.x).
- **Impact on Fish Health:** Both live and dead fish shed *F. psychrophilum* into the water, but dead fish shed the bacterium at substantially higher rates and for longer durations than live fish, meaning that mortality events feed forward into further transmission pressure within a population if not managed promptly [13](https://doi.org/10.1128/spectrum.03601-23). In Chile's national surveillance, flavobacteriosis was the leading infectious cause of freshwater mortality in rainbow trout and coho salmon in 2025 [3](https://www.sernapesca.cl/app/uploads/2026/03/Informe-Situacion-Sanitaria-Salmonicultura-Ano-2025.pdf), and the leading reason for antimicrobial use in the freshwater phase [14](https://www.sernapesca.cl/app/uploads/2026/03/Informe-de-uso-de-antimicrobianos-ano-2025.pdf).

### Causes of Bacterial Cold-Water Disease (*Flavobacterium psychrophilum*)

**Aetiology**

- **Causative Agent:** *F. psychrophilum* is a Gram-negative rod (members of the genus measure 0.3–0.5 μm in diameter) that is non-flagellated and either non-motile or displays gliding motility; it is fastidious and generally does not grow on standard media such as trypticase soy agar [1](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jare.2014.10.009). It shows limited genomic diversity worldwide, with the MLST clonal complex CC-ST10 being the main clonal complex infecting farmed rainbow trout worldwide [15](https://doi.org/10.3389/fmicb.2018.00138).
- **Key Virulence Factors:** Colony spreading motility and proteolytic activity are required for pathogenicity. In a comparative study, the only non-virulent isolate tested was deficient in both traits [16](https://doi.org/10.3389/fmicb.2019.01711). The *gldD* and *gldG* genes, part of the gliding-motility/Type IX secretion system (T9SS), are required for adhesion, biofilm formation, proteolytic activity, and full virulence; mutants deficient in either gene showed significantly reduced virulence in rainbow trout challenge models [17](https://doi.org/10.3389/fmicb.2017.02168).
- **Transmission Methods:**
    - **Horizontal Transmission:** Both live and dead fish shed *F. psychrophilum* into the surrounding water; dead fish shed the bacterium at markedly higher rates and for longer durations than live fish, and shedding dynamics vary by bacterial genetic variant and host species [13](https://doi.org/10.1128/spectrum.03601-23).
    - **Vertical / Egg-Associated Transmission:** *F. psychrophilum* has been isolated from milt, ovarian fluids, and in close association with eggs, supporting a vertical transmission route that operates alongside horizontal transmission [18](https://doi.org/10.3390/microorganisms9050971). Epidemic genotypes have also been isolated from eggs and sexual fluids, so trade in eggs and gametes, not only live fish, can carry the bacterium to new regions [19](https://doi.org/10.1128/AEM.04233-13).

**Risk Factors**

- **Environmental Factors:** In one experimental study, raising water temperature to 18 °C shortened the time to clinical disease, an unexpected result since outbreaks are typically reported at colder temperatures. In the same study, 1% salinity delayed onset and improved cohabitant survival (42.6% vs 17.9%) [20](https://doi.org/10.1111/jfd.70037). In the field, cases in coho salmon during the estuarine grow-out phase are explained by freshwater from rivers and rainfall lowering salinity. This helps infections that started in fresh water persist and spread horizontally, and creates conditions that favour new infections [11](https://marcosgodoy.com/flavobacteriosis-en-salmon-coho-oncorhynchus-kisutch-en-estuario-patologia-macroscopica/).
- **Farm Management Practices:** Handling and stress cause immunosuppression, and handling also causes skin lesions through which the bacterium can enter. In one Montana hatchery, outbreaks typically occurred three weeks after fish were handled or moved. Poor water quality and malnutrition have also been linked to outbreaks, and rainbow trout fed a diet high in oxidised lipids had higher mortality after challenge [10](https://doi.org/10.2174/1874401X01104010040). Dead fish shed far more bacteria than live fish, so mortalities left in tanks add to infection pressure [13](https://doi.org/10.1128/spectrum.03601-23).

### Diagnosis

**Diagnostic Methods**

- **Clinical Examination:** Diagnosis relies mainly on gross signs, microscopic examination of fresh spleen samples, and culture. Because lesions can be superficial and the bacterium grows slowly, early infections are easily missed [21](https://doi.org/10.1186/1471-2180-14-105). Clinical signs also overlap with other bacterial diseases (see Differential Diagnosis), so laboratory confirmation is required.
- **Laboratory Tests:**
    - **Tissues to Sample:** In clinical cases, the spleen is the main target organ. By qPCR, the bacterium was found in spleens of healthy fish too, but reached quantifiable levels only in spleens of diseased fish [21](https://doi.org/10.1186/1471-2180-14-105). For culture and PCR, the spleen, head kidney, and liver of moribund or dead fish are used [22](https://doi.org/10.3390/microorganisms11102546), and the bacterium was first isolated from the kidney and external lesions [1](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jare.2014.10.009). Brain tissue is also used for PCR, where nested PCR was more sensitive than agar culture [23](https://doi.org/10.1046/j.1365-2672.2000.00959.x). For broodstock screening, kidney and ovarian fluid are sampled (see Immunological Methods).
    - **Bacterial Culture:** *F. psychrophilum* is fastidious and requires specialised low-nutrient media (e.g., TYES or Shieh's medium); it does not grow on trypticase soy agar [1](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jare.2014.10.009). Tryptone-yeast extract-salts agar with added glucose was the most effective medium for isolation from diseased fish tissue at 18 °C, with incubation times of about 72 h. Optimal growth is at 18–20 °C, with no growth at 30 °C or above [10](https://doi.org/10.2174/1874401X01104010040).
    - **PCR-Based Methods:** A quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) assay targeting the *rpoC* gene allows highly sensitive and specific detection and quantification of *F. psychrophilum* directly from water and fish tissue samples without the need for culturing [21](https://doi.org/10.1186/1471-2180-14-105). A multiplex PCR assay enables simultaneous, accurate differentiation of *F. psychrophilum* from two other major salmonid pathogens, *Yersinia ruckeri* and *Aeromonas salmonicida* subsp. *salmonicida*, in a single reaction [24](https://doi.org/10.4142/jvs.2010.11.3.235).
    - **Immunological Methods:** ELISA and fluorescent antibody techniques are used for both identification and screening [10](https://doi.org/10.2174/1874401X01104010040). To screen broodstock before culling eggs from heavily infected parents, kidney ELISA was far more sensitive than kidney culture (diagnostic sensitivity 0.97 vs 0.02) and also detected subclinically infected fish [25](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-2761.2012.01357.x).
    - **Environmental DNA/RNA (Water Sampling):** In a 12-week study of two freshwater recirculating aquaculture system (RAS) units on a commercial Atlantic salmon pre-smolt farm, *F. psychrophilum* was detected in water samples at higher and more stable levels than in gill swabs, which showed only minor fluctuations. This supports non-lethal water sampling as a way to track the bacterium in RAS, although a positive water sample does not by itself indicate active infection, and no clinical *F. psychrophilum* disease was observed during the study [26](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.aquaculture.2025.743060).

**Differential Diagnosis**

- **Other Flavobacterial Diseases:**
    - **Columnaris disease** (*F. columnare*): also produces yellowish skin lesions, eroded fins, and gill necrosis, but outbreaks have historically been associated with summer, whereas *F. psychrophilum* frequently causes disease below 10 °C [1](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jare.2014.10.009).
    - **Bacterial gill disease** (*F. branchiophilum*): damage is confined to the gills (bacterial growth over the gill surface, excess mucus, and fused lamellae), with fish otherwise appearing normal until death [1](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jare.2014.10.009). Affected fish show increased opercular movements, gasping at the surface, and respiratory distress [27](https://doi.org/10.3390/ani11010125).
    - **Other yellow-pigmented flavobacteria:** previously uncharacterized *Flavobacterium* and *Chryseobacterium* species have caused lesions very similar to BCWD, including erosion of the adipose fin, multifocal ulcers, and exophthalmia [1](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jare.2014.10.009). Telling them apart requires laboratory identification, and MALDI-TOF mass spectrometry has been used to differentiate *F. psychrophilum* from *F. psychrophilum*-like species [27](https://doi.org/10.3390/ani11010125).
    - **Tenacibaculosis** ([[Tenacibaculosis (Tenacibaculum spp)]]): causes skin erosions, ulcers, and eroded fins, head, and gills [27](https://doi.org/10.3390/ani11010125). It should be considered when ulcers appear after transfer to brackish or sea water, such as in coho salmon in estuaries [11](https://marcosgodoy.com/flavobacteriosis-en-salmon-coho-oncorhynchus-kisutch-en-estuario-patologia-macroscopica/).
- **Diseases with Overlapping Signs:**
    - **Yersiniosis** ([[Yersiniosis (enteric redmouth disease)]]): lethargy, darkening of the skin, exophthalmia, and haemorrhages in and around the mouth and throat [28](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13567-015-0238-4).
    - **Furunculosis** ([[Furunculosis (Aeromonas salmonicida)]]): lethargy, darkened skin, exophthalmia, haemorrhages at the base of the fins, enlarged spleen, and skin lesions and ulcers [27](https://doi.org/10.3390/ani11010125).
    - **Viral diseases of fry:** infectious pancreatic necrosis ([[Infectious Pancreatic Necrosis (IPN)]]) and infectious haematopoietic necrosis (IHN) both cause darkened skin and exophthalmia, with irregular swimming in IPN and lethargy in IHN [27](https://doi.org/10.3390/ani11010125).
    - **Whirling disease** (*Myxobolus cerebralis*): causes blacktail, spinal and cranial deformities, and exophthalmia in young rainbow trout. BCWD can also produce blacktail, spiral swimming, and spinal deformities, and both pathogens can be present in the same hatchery [29](https://doi.org/10.1111/jfd.13605).
    - **Saprolegniasis** ([[Saprolegniasis]]): a frequent cause of skin lesions in fresh water. In Chile, mycosis was the leading infectious cause of freshwater mortality in Atlantic salmon in 2025 [3](https://www.sernapesca.cl/app/uploads/2026/03/Informe-Situacion-Sanitaria-Salmonicultura-Ano-2025.pdf).
- **Confirming the Diagnosis:** Because these signs overlap, diagnosis relies on culture on low-nutrient media and PCR (see Laboratory Tests above). A multiplex PCR assay allows *F. psychrophilum*, *Y. ruckeri* and *A. salmonicida* subsp. *salmonicida* to be identified from the same sample [24](https://doi.org/10.4142/jvs.2010.11.3.235).

### Treatment and Prevention

**Treatment Options**

- **Current Treatments:**
    - **Antibiotics:** Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) profiling of *F. psychrophilum* shows a consistent pattern across geographically distant farming regions: florfenicol retains high effectiveness, while resistance to oxytetracycline and quinolones is widespread. In Slovenia, 86.3% of isolates were non-wild-type (reduced susceptibility) for oxytetracycline and enrofloxacin and 90.2% for oxolinic acid, while all isolates were wild-type for florfenicol and erythromycin; reduced oxolinic acid susceptibility was linked to a T83A or T83V substitution in the *gyrA* gene [5](https://doi.org/10.1111/jfd.14119). In Chile, MIC-based testing found non-wild-type frequencies of 70% for oxytetracycline and 38–45% for the quinolones oxolinic acid, flumequine, and enrofloxacin, but only 2% for florfenicol (8% by disk diffusion) [30](https://doi.org/10.3389/fmicb.2016.01880). Florfenicol susceptibility is not universal, however: an earlier Chilean study using a different interpretive cut-off (normalized resistance interpretation, non-susceptible at >2.35 µg/mL) classified 92.5% of 40 isolates as resistant to florfenicol [31](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.aquaculture.2012.04.034), and reduced florfenicol susceptibility has also been reported in 52.8% of Canadian isolates, alongside field reports of lost treatment efficacy in Chile [5](https://doi.org/10.1111/jfd.14119).

- **Vaccines:**
    - **Subunit and Attenuated Candidates:** Vaccine development has been hampered by the bacterium's antigenic and genetic diversity. Candidate antigens (OmpH, OmpA, FspA, and lipopolysaccharide) have been identified using sera from convalescent rainbow trout, and an attenuated *exbD2* mutant strain achieved 81.8% relative percent survival (RPS) after intramuscular immunization and challenge [32](https://doi.org/10.1111/1751-7915.12099).
    - **Polyvalent Immersion Vaccine:** A polyvalent immersion vaccine combining three formalin-inactivated *F. psychrophilum* strains gave 84% RPS in rainbow trout fry against a heterologous strain challenge, with vaccinated fish also showing a delayed mortality onset compared to unvaccinated controls [6](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13567-017-0448-z).
    - **Vaccine Implementation:** Commercial vaccines exist in only a few countries, and none suit the juvenile fry that bear the highest disease burden, so antibiotics remain the main response to outbreaks [6](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13567-017-0448-z). In Chile, the Agricultural and Livestock Service (SAG) lists FLAVOMUNE (Veterquímica, immersion) and ALPHA JECT IPNV-Flavo 0,025 (Pharmaq, injection) [33](https://sag.gob.cl/sites/default/files/lista_salmonidos_registro_provisional_03122024.pdf). In Norway, the combination vaccine Flavo AVM6 (Pharmaq) is available for rainbow trout [34](https://www.dmp.no/en/veterinary-medicine/fish-medicine/approved-medicines-for-use-in-fish). Injectable vaccination works well in larger fish, but no immersion vaccine is on the market [4](https://www.vetinst.no/rapporter-og-publikasjoner/rapporter/2026/fiskehelserapporten-2025).
    - **Autogenous Vaccines:** Because several serotypes and antigenic groups can co-circulate within a single farm, and each farm tends to have its own dominant serotype, autogenous (site-specific) vaccines built on each farm's dominant isolates have been recommended for Chilean salmonid farms [35](https://doi.org/10.1111/jfd.13828).

- **Experimental Alternatives:**
    - **Phage-Derived Endolysins (in vitro only):** Ely174, an endolysin from an *F. psychrophilum* bacteriophage, lysed *F. psychrophilum* in vitro once the outer membrane had been permeabilised. Protein engineering improved its activity and thermal stability, and fusing it with the C-terminal domain of the Type IX secretion system protein SprA let it lyse untreated *F. psychrophilum* cells without an outer-membrane permeabiliser, although slowly and only at high concentrations [36](https://doi.org/10.1128/aem.01891-25).

**Preventive Measures**

- **Biosecurity Protocols:**
    - Iodine-based egg disinfection, standard in salmonid hatcheries, reduces surface bacterial load but does not sterilise eggs, and intra-ovum infection has been reported [18](https://doi.org/10.3390/microorganisms9050971). *F. psychrophilum* is very iodine tolerant, surviving at least 100 mg/L active iodine for 30 minutes, and ultraviolet (UV) disinfection needs doses of 126 mWs/cm² or more, since 42 mWs/cm² does not kill it [10](https://doi.org/10.2174/1874401X01104010040).
    - Experimental: bacteriophages showed short-term (24 h) control of *F. psychrophilum* on eyed eggs without affecting egg survival [18](https://doi.org/10.3390/microorganisms9050971).
    - Prompt removal of dead and moribund fish to reduce the disproportionate shedding load they contribute to hatchery and farm water [13](https://doi.org/10.1128/spectrum.03601-23).
    - Using pathogen-free water, from protected sources or treated by filtration, UV, or ozonation [10](https://doi.org/10.2174/1874401X01104010040).

- **Farm Management Practices:**
    - Managing water temperature and salinity where feasible, which experimentally influenced RTFS onset and fry survival [20](https://doi.org/10.1111/jfd.70037).
    - Avoiding or minimising handling and stress, and reducing rearing densities [10](https://doi.org/10.2174/1874401X01104010040).
    - Keeping water quality high (low organic load and nitrite) and feeding high-quality diets [10](https://doi.org/10.2174/1874401X01104010040).
    - Screening broodstock, for example by ELISA and filtration-based fluorescent antibody tests, although this may be of little use if the rearing environment is already heavily contaminated [10](https://doi.org/10.2174/1874401X01104010040).

- **Genetic Selection:**
    - Selective breeding for BCWD resistance is already applied in commercial rainbow trout lines. In one important breeding population, adding genomic information raised prediction accuracy for resistance from 0.27 (pedigree-based) to 0.36. The trait behaved as oligogenic there, with its largest association peaks on chromosomes Omy25 and Omy8 [37](https://doi.org/10.3389/fgene.2025.1582138).
    - Resistance architecture can differ between populations. In two French commercial rainbow trout populations, 29-day survival after bath challenge was 71.3% vs 50.7%, and the 11 QTLs detected showed limited overlap between the two populations. Complement-system genes (e.g., *C3*, *Cfb*) sat in two of the QTL regions. Breeding programmes may therefore need population-specific genetic markers [38](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12864-025-12231-3).

### Case Studies

**Real-World Examples**

- **Notable Outbreaks:**
    - **Arrival of an Epidemic Clone in Rainbow Trout (Norway, 2008 onwards):** In Norway, *F. psychrophilum* was considered a minor problem in brown trout and salmon hatcheries until 2008, when an epidemic of systemic disease caused high mortalities on several rainbow trout farms. Of 117 Norwegian isolates typed by MLST, 46% belonged to clonal complex CC-ST10, and ST2 was the only CC-ST10 genotype found in the country. Given Norway's short (<10-year) history of BCWD in rainbow trout, this points to the recent introduction of an epidemic clone. CC-ST10 genotypes have been isolated from juvenile and adult fish, eggs, and sexual fluids, so trade in live fish, eggs, and gametes can all spread the clone [19](https://doi.org/10.1128/AEM.04233-13). The disease went on to cause high mortality in fry and small fish at several Norwegian rainbow trout hatcheries, and also caused disease in large rainbow trout in low-salinity areas [4](https://www.vetinst.no/rapporter-og-publikasjoner/rapporter/2026/fiskehelserapporten-2025).
    - **First Atlantic Salmon Case (Norway, Winter 2008–2009):** The first Norwegian case of increased mortality in Atlantic salmon occurred in 60–100 g smolts in fresh water on a land-based farm in Western Norway, at water temperatures below 5 °C, with 7.0% accumulated mortality. Affected fish showed septicaemia and necrotic myositis (swollen dark spleen, pale liver, serohaemorrhagic ascites, and haemorrhage in abdominal fat and muscle), and two different genotypes of the bacterium were present in the same outbreak [9](https://doi.org/10.3354/dao02390).
    - **Mixed Strains Within the Same Outbreak (Chile, 2018–2019):** *F. psychrophilum* was isolated from diseased Atlantic salmon and rainbow trout on five geographically separated freshwater farms (68 isolates). Each farm had a predominant serotype (types 0 and 4 in Atlantic salmon, types 1 and 2 in rainbow trout), but several antigenic groups co-existed within some farms during the same outbreak, and 71% of isolates carried the virulent CSF259-93 16S rRNA allele. In experimental challenges, type-2 isolates from rainbow trout were the most pathogenic [35](https://doi.org/10.1111/jfd.13828).
    - **Infected Broodstock and Hatchery Losses (Great Lakes Basin, United States):** A 5-year study of Michigan broodstocks detected *F. psychrophilum* in all broodstocks except captive lake trout and brook trout. Prevalence was highest in feral Chinook salmon from the Lake Michigan watershed (mean 63.2%), and captive broodstocks had significantly lower prevalence than feral ones. Despite this, the bacterium was rarely detected in the progeny during hatchery rearing, yet heavy losses from clinical BCWD outbreaks still occurred [39](https://doi.org/10.1080/08997659.2015.1088488).

- **Lessons Learned:** The Norwegian experience shows how quickly a single epidemic clone can establish itself once introduced, and that the movement of eggs, gametes, and live fish is a key route of spread [19](https://doi.org/10.1128/AEM.04233-13). Good biosecurity, egg disinfection, and avoiding the movement of infected fish to new areas have kept systemic infection in Norwegian rainbow trout to a handful of sites per year [4](https://www.vetinst.no/rapporter-og-publikasjoner/rapporter/2026/fiskehelserapporten-2025). The Chilean data show that one outbreak can involve several serotypes at once, which complicates vaccine design and led the authors to recommend autogenous (site-specific) vaccines built on each farm's dominant isolates [35](https://doi.org/10.1111/jfd.13828). The Great Lakes study shows that broodstock infection status alone does not predict which hatchery cohorts will suffer outbreaks [39](https://doi.org/10.1080/08997659.2015.1088488).

## Data Insights

### Disease Impact by Country

#### Norway

- **Incidence:**
    - Systemic infection with *F. psychrophilum* in rainbow trout is a notifiable disease in Norway (national list, category F). It was confirmed at 1, 4, 1, 1, and 2 sites in 2021, 2022, 2023, 2024, and 2025, respectively. The two 2025 rainbow trout sites involved sequence types ST169 (a highly diverse group thought to include environmental variants) and ST187 (previously found in skin ulcers of Atlantic salmon). In the fjord system where the bacterium had been found in recent years, no disease was detected in large rainbow trout in 2025 [4](https://www.vetinst.no/rapporter-og-publikasjoner/rapporter/2026/fiskehelserapporten-2025).
    - In Atlantic salmon, *F. psychrophilum* was detected at 14 sites spread along the coast (production areas PO3–PO13) in 2025, on four in ten sites in fish recorded as healthy and on three in ten in association with disease. At a broodstock facility with skin ulcers, one isolate belonged to ST15, not previously found in Norway. The disease can still be a challenge in the freshwater (smolt production) phase [4](https://www.vetinst.no/rapporter-og-publikasjoner/rapporter/2026/fiskehelserapporten-2025).

- **Treatment & Management:**
    - No immersion vaccine is on the Norwegian market. An available injectable vaccine for larger fish works well, and a combination vaccine containing *F. psychrophilum* is used actively in the area exposed to the disease. General biosecurity, egg disinfection to reduce possible vertical transmission, and avoiding the movement of infected fish to new areas are the main control measures [4](https://www.vetinst.no/rapporter-og-publikasjoner/rapporter/2026/fiskehelserapporten-2025).

#### Slovenia

- **Incidence:**
    - A survey of Slovenian farmed rainbow trout confirmed the presence of *F. psychrophilum* (51 isolates characterized) [5](https://doi.org/10.1111/jfd.14119).

- **Treatment & Management:**
    - 86.3% of isolates showed reduced susceptibility to oxytetracycline and enrofloxacin, and 90.2% to oxolinic acid, while all isolates were wild-type for florfenicol and erythromycin [5](https://doi.org/10.1111/jfd.14119).

#### Chile

- **Incidence:**
    - *F. psychrophilum* is described as the most important bacterial pathogen of freshwater farmed salmonids in Chile, with 125 isolates characterized from reared salmonids showing clinical signs of disease [30](https://doi.org/10.3389/fmicb.2016.01880).
    - In 2025, flavobacteriosis accounted for 95.1% of freshwater mortality attributed to infectious causes in rainbow trout, 51.9% in coho salmon, and 24.9% in Atlantic salmon (where mycosis led with 60.2%). These shares refer to infectious mortality only, which is a minority of total freshwater mortality (8.4% in Atlantic salmon, where productive culling was the main recorded cause). *F. psychrophilum* was also the second most frequently detected pathogen in positive diagnostic reports from freshwater farms (17%), after piscine orthoreovirus (PRV, 36.9%) [3](https://www.sernapesca.cl/app/uploads/2026/03/Informe-Situacion-Sanitaria-Salmonicultura-Ano-2025.pdf).

- **Treatment & Management:**
    - Flavobacteriosis was the leading reason for antimicrobial use in Chilean freshwater salmonid farming in 2025, accounting for 46.65% of freshwater use, ahead of renibacteriosis (BKD, 30.96%) and furunculosis (7.35%). Freshwater treatments relied on oxytetracycline (60.63%) and florfenicol (39.15%), mostly given in feed but also by immersion, and immersion treatments against flavobacteriosis drive the high use in the Los Ríos region. The freshwater phase accounted for only 1.17% of national antimicrobial use, and freshwater use fell by 34.1% compared with the previous year [14](https://www.sernapesca.cl/app/uploads/2026/03/Informe-de-uso-de-antimicrobianos-ano-2025.pdf).
    - MIC-based epidemiological cut-off analysis found non-wild-type frequencies of 70% for oxytetracycline and 38–45% for the quinolones oxolinic acid, flumequine, and enrofloxacin, while only 2% of isolates (8% by disk diffusion) showed reduced susceptibility to florfenicol. This resistance pattern is broadly consistent with the Slovenian data above despite the geographic distance between the two surveys [30](https://doi.org/10.3389/fmicb.2016.01880). An earlier study of 40 Chilean isolates, using a different interpretive cut-off, reported resistance in 90% to oxytetracycline, 92.5% to florfenicol, and 85% to oxolinic acid, and linked these levels to the large quantities of antimicrobials used to control *F. psychrophilum* outbreaks in Chilean farms [31](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.aquaculture.2012.04.034).

#### United States

- **Incidence:**
    - The disease was first described in the United States, in rainbow trout in West Virginia and juvenile coho salmon in Washington [1](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jare.2014.10.009). In Michigan's Great Lakes basin, *F. psychrophilum* was detected in almost all salmonid broodstocks sampled over five years (see Case Studies) [39](https://doi.org/10.1080/08997659.2015.1088488).

- **Treatment & Management:**
    - Nitrofurans and sulfonamides are not registered for use in food fish in the United States, and florfenicol was approved only shortly before 2011 [10](https://doi.org/10.2174/1874401X01104010040).

#### Canada

- **Incidence:**
    - BCWD has a considerable economic impact on aquaculture in Ontario, where 75 isolates were collected over 16 years from farmed salmonids with tail rot, necrotic myositis, and osteochondrosis [40](https://doi.org/10.1139/w08-058).

- **Treatment & Management:**
    - Reduced susceptibility to florfenicol has been reported in 52.8% of Canadian isolates [5](https://doi.org/10.1111/jfd.14119).

#### Denmark

- **Incidence:**
    - BCWD caused an average mortality of 34% in Danish rainbow trout farms [10](https://doi.org/10.2174/1874401X01104010040).

- **Treatment & Management:**
    - Oxolinic acid was used from 1986, but by 2000 all isolates were resistant, and 60 to 75% of isolates from 1994 to 1998 were resistant to oxytetracycline [10](https://doi.org/10.2174/1874401X01104010040). Florfenicol is now the drug of choice. Between 2019 and 2022, two isolates from a farm where florfenicol treatment failed showed reduced susceptibility and carried the *floR* gene. They belonged to a clonal complex (CC-ST90) not previously reported in the Nordic countries [41](https://doi.org/10.1111/jfd.70255).

#### China

- **Incidence:**
    - The first published report linked *F. psychrophilum* to outbreaks in salmonid farms in four provinces, involving three host species. Isolates belonged to CC-ST10, common on other continents, and to three sequence types not reported elsewhere [42](https://doi.org/10.1128/spectrum.00330-21). It has also been isolated from farmed cyprinids with overwintering mortality syndrome [7](https://doi.org/10.3390/ani16101465) [8](https://doi.org/10.3389/fmicb.2026.1751566).

- **Treatment & Management:**
    - Chinese isolates showed reduced susceptibility to enrofloxacin, flumequine, and oxolinic acid, and moderate susceptibility to gentamicin, erythromycin, and florfenicol [42](https://doi.org/10.1128/spectrum.00330-21).

#### Japan

- **Incidence:**
    - Besides salmonids, *F. psychrophilum* frequently affects ayu (*Plecoglossus altivelis*) in Japanese farms and rivers, causing heavy mortality and severe economic losses. Ayu isolates belong to sequence types distinct from those in salmonids, whereas isolates from Japanese coho salmon and rainbow trout match those found in the same species abroad [43](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13567-024-01326-6).

#### South Korea

- **Incidence:**
    - The disease was not documented in Korean rainbow trout before September 2018. The first isolate was closely related to European strains, suggesting introduction through imported fertilised eggs [22](https://doi.org/10.3390/microorganisms11102546). The bacterium has also been isolated from farmed ayu in Korea [43](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13567-024-01326-6).

- **Treatment & Management:**
    - In the first Korean outbreak, oral enrofloxacin reduced mortality from 28% to 6%. The isolate was resistant to oxolinic acid and sulfamethoxazole/trimethoprim [22](https://doi.org/10.3390/microorganisms11102546).

## Conclusion

*F. psychrophilum* causes substantial, recurring losses in cold-water salmonid hatcheries worldwide. The most effective management strategies currently available combine water-temperature and salinity management, prompt removal of dead and moribund fish to limit shedding, iodine-based egg disinfection to reduce egg-associated bacterial load, and judicious antibiotic use guided by local resistance surveillance. Florfenicol remains the most consistently effective option in recent surveys from geographically distant farming regions (Slovenia, Chile), even where resistance to oxytetracycline and quinolones is already widespread, although reduced florfenicol susceptibility has been reported in Chile and Canada. No commercially robust, broadly available vaccine yet exists, and none of the limited vaccines that do exist are suitable for the juvenile fry that bear the greatest disease burden, leaving antibiotics as the default response to most outbreaks. Genomic selection for BCWD resistance in rainbow trout offers a complementary, longer-term route, although resistance loci can differ between breeding populations.

Bacteriophage-based biocontrol, while still preliminary, merits continued investigation as a non-antibiotic intervention, particularly around the egg stage where vertical transmission can be intercepted. Documented outbreaks from Norway, Chile, and the Great Lakes point to the same practical lessons: epidemic clones spread through the trade in eggs, gametes, and live fish; several strains can co-circulate within a single farm, which complicates vaccine design; and sustained biosecurity can keep the disease to a handful of sites per year, as Norway's official surveillance shows. Country-level surveillance data nonetheless remains sparse outside of the few farming regions covered here.
