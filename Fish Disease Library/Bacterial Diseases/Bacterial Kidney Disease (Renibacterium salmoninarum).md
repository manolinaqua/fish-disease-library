## Overview

### What is Bacterial Kidney Disease (Renibacterium salmoninarum)?

Bacterial Kidney Disease (BKD) is a chronic, systemic bacterial infection of salmonid fish caused by *Renibacterium salmoninarum*. It is characterised by granulomatous lesions (white nodules) in the kidney and other organs. Wild and farmed salmonids can be affected at all life stages, in both freshwater and seawater, and the disease has a serious impact on the salmonid industry [3](https://doi.org/10.3390/pathogens9100845). There is no effective cure or fully protective vaccine, and infected fish can remain lifelong carriers, so control relies on keeping the bacterium out of broodstock and farms [7](https://www.vetinst.no/sykdom-og-agens/bakteriell-nyresyke-bkd).

**Causative Pathogen**

*R. salmoninarum* is a small, Gram-positive, non-motile, non-spore-forming, aerobic diplobacillus (0.3–1.0 × 1.0–1.5 µm). It was first placed in the genus *Corynebacterium* but differs from it in cell wall composition, and it is now the only species in the genus *Renibacterium*. Its closest relatives are environmental *Arthrobacter* species [3](https://doi.org/10.3390/pathogens9100845). The bacterium is fastidious and slow-growing: it grows at 15–18 °C on cysteine-supplemented media such as kidney disease medium (KDM-2) or selective KDM (SKDM), and colonies take 2–6 weeks, sometimes 8–12 weeks, to appear [3](https://doi.org/10.3390/pathogens9100845) [7](https://www.vetinst.no/sykdom-og-agens/bakteriell-nyresyke-bkd). It survives inside host cells (facultative intracellular), which helps explain why antibiotics and vaccines perform poorly. Its best-characterised virulence factor is a 57 kDa surface protein (p57, also called major soluble antigen, MSA), which suppresses the host immune response [3](https://doi.org/10.3390/pathogens9100845). The same protein is the target of the ELISA test (see Diagnosis).

**Host Species**

- **Salmonids:** All members of the family Salmonidae are considered susceptible [3](https://doi.org/10.3390/pathogens9100845). Species reported include Atlantic salmon (*Salmo salar*), rainbow trout (*Oncorhynchus mykiss*), brown/sea trout (*Salmo trutta*), Arctic charr (*Salvelinus alpinus*), brook trout (*Salvelinus fontinalis*), grayling (*Thymallus thymallus*) and the Pacific salmon (*Oncorhynchus* spp.) [3](https://doi.org/10.3390/pathogens9100845) [7](https://www.vetinst.no/sykdom-og-agens/bakteriell-nyresyke-bkd). Pink (*O. gorbuscha*), sockeye (*O. nerka*) and Chinook salmon (*O. tshawytscha*) are particularly susceptible compared with Atlantic salmon and rainbow trout [3](https://doi.org/10.3390/pathogens9100845). In 2024 the disease was reported for the first time in farmed chum salmon (*O. keta*) in South Korea [17](https://doi.org/10.3390/microorganisms12112329).
- **Non-salmonid fish (natural infection or detection):** Ayu (*Plecoglossus altivelis*) has been naturally infected. The bacterium has also been detected in European eel in England, in herring (*Clupea harengus*), in sea lamprey (*Petromyzon marinus*) in Lake Ontario, and in flounder, greenling, Japanese sculpin and flathead caught around Japanese coho salmon sea cages [3](https://doi.org/10.3390/pathogens9100845).
- **Non-salmonid fish (experimental infection):** Pacific herring (*Clupea pallasii*), sablefish (*Anoplopoma fimbria*), shiner perch (*Cymatogaster aggregata*), common shiner (*Notropis cornutus*) and fathead minnow (*Pimephales promelas*) developed disease after experimental infection [3](https://doi.org/10.3390/pathogens9100845). Carp (*Cyprinus carpio*), by contrast, cleared experimental infection [16](https://doi.org/10.3389/fimmu.2021.733266).
- **Cleaner fish:** Lumpfish (*Cyclopterus lumpus*), widely stocked with Atlantic salmon to control sea lice, are susceptible to experimental infection. At the highest dose (10⁹ cells per fish), cumulative mortality was 35%, and bacteria persisted in spleen, liver and head kidney for at least 98 days. The authors suggest lumpfish could act as carriers, although transmission between lumpfish and salmon has not yet been demonstrated [16](https://doi.org/10.3389/fimmu.2021.733266).
- **Invertebrates:** Blue mussels (*Mytilus edulis*) filter the bacterium from seawater and scallops (*Patinopecten yessoensis*) can carry it, so both may act as reservoirs near farms [3](https://doi.org/10.3390/pathogens9100845).

**Origin and Geographical Distribution**

BKD was first described in the 1930s in wild Atlantic salmon from the rivers Dee and Spey in Scotland, which gave it the early name "Dee disease". In 1935 a similar disease was recorded in brook, brown and rainbow trout in North America, where a Gram-positive bacterium was seen in kidney lesions but could not yet be cultured [3](https://doi.org/10.3390/pathogens9100845) [6](https://thefishsite.com/disease-guide/bacterial-kidney-disease-bkd). Other historical names include corynebacterial kidney disease, white boil disease and salmonid kidney disease [3](https://doi.org/10.3390/pathogens9100845).

Today BKD is reported from North America, South America (notably Chile), Europe and East Asia (Japan and South Korea) [3](https://doi.org/10.3390/pathogens9100845) [17](https://doi.org/10.3390/microorganisms12112329). In Europe it has been recorded in, among others, Scotland, Norway (first detected in 1980) and Sweden (first diagnosed in 1985) [4](https://doi.org/10.3354/dao059125) [7](https://www.vetinst.no/sykdom-og-agens/bakteriell-nyresyke-bkd) [18](https://doi.org/10.1111/jfd.13614). It has not been reported from Ireland, Australia or New Zealand [3](https://doi.org/10.3390/pathogens9100845).

Movement of live fish and eggs is the main route of international spread. Japan's BKD is believed to have arrived with imported coho salmon eggs certified as infection-free [5](https://food.ec.europa.eu/document/download/e0fa9624-88b7-438a-83a0-294a2622d593_en?filename=sci-com_scah_out36_en.pdf), and whole-genome sequencing of Chilean isolates collected between 2012 and 2016 grouped them into four clusters, consistent with several separate introductions from different countries [3](https://doi.org/10.3390/pathogens9100845).

## Clinical Signs of Bacterial Kidney Disease (Renibacterium salmoninarum)

### Common Signs

- **Physical Signs:**
    - **Kidney lesions:** Swollen, pale and granular kidney with large white nodules that may merge [1](https://www.adfg.alaska.gov/static/species/disease/pdfs/fishdiseases/bacterial_kidney_disease.pdf) [7](https://www.vetinst.no/sykdom-og-agens/bakteriell-nyresyke-bkd).
    - **White nodules in other organs:** Numerous small nodules (<2 mm) in other internal organs, most visible in dark organs such as the spleen. The heart, spleen and liver may also be enlarged [3](https://doi.org/10.3390/pathogens9100845) [7](https://www.vetinst.no/sykdom-og-agens/bakteriell-nyresyke-bkd).
    - **Anaemia:** Pale gills and internal organs [7](https://www.vetinst.no/sykdom-og-agens/bakteriell-nyresyke-bkd).
    - **Exophthalmia:** Protruding eyes, caused by kidney damage disrupting osmoregulation in freshwater, or by inflammation behind the eye in sea-phase fish [7](https://www.vetinst.no/sykdom-og-agens/bakteriell-nyresyke-bkd).
    - **Skin lesions:** Small skin haemorrhages early in infection that can develop into small crater-like ulcers, and fluid-filled blisters (the origin of the name "white boil disease") [3](https://doi.org/10.3390/pathogens9100845) [7](https://www.vetinst.no/sykdom-og-agens/bakteriell-nyresyke-bkd). Sexually mature rainbow trout and charr may show a transient spawning rash [7](https://www.vetinst.no/sykdom-og-agens/bakteriell-nyresyke-bkd).
    - **Haemorrhages:** Petechiae on the skin, fins and internal organs [1](https://www.adfg.alaska.gov/static/species/disease/pdfs/fishdiseases/bacterial_kidney_disease.pdf) [2](https://www.glfc.org/pubs/SpecialPubs/sp83_2/pdf/chap21.pdf).
    - **Abdominal swelling:** Distended abdomen with ascites (sometimes bloody) and enlarged organs [3](https://doi.org/10.3390/pathogens9100845) [7](https://www.vetinst.no/sykdom-og-agens/bakteriell-nyresyke-bkd).
    - **Fibrin membranes:** At low water temperatures, chronic cases develop a whitish fibrin layer over the abdominal organs, described as "cotton wool" [3](https://doi.org/10.3390/pathogens9100845) [7](https://www.vetinst.no/sykdom-og-agens/bakteriell-nyresyke-bkd).

- **Behavioural Signs:**
    - **Lethargy and loss of appetite:** Reduced activity, reduced feeding and weight loss [2](https://www.glfc.org/pubs/SpecialPubs/sp83_2/pdf/chap21.pdf).
    - **Abnormal swimming:** Loss of equilibrium and erratic swimming. In Norwegian sea farms, affected fish are often found among the "svimere" (fish that swim erratically or lose balance) [3](https://doi.org/10.3390/pathogens9100845) [7](https://www.vetinst.no/sykdom-og-agens/bakteriell-nyresyke-bkd).

**Progression of Clinical Signs**

- **Early stages:** Subtle signs such as reduced feed intake and slight lethargy [2](https://www.glfc.org/pubs/SpecialPubs/sp83_2/pdf/chap21.pdf). Typical lesions may be absent at necropsy because they take time to develop [7](https://www.vetinst.no/sykdom-og-agens/bakteriell-nyresyke-bkd).
- **Advanced stages:** Swollen kidneys, white nodules in the kidney and other organs, skin lesions, ascites and haemorrhages, followed by mortality [1](https://www.adfg.alaska.gov/static/species/disease/pdfs/fishdiseases/bacterial_kidney_disease.pdf) [7](https://www.vetinst.no/sykdom-og-agens/bakteriell-nyresyke-bkd). In the brain, the bacterium can cause encephalitis and meningitis [3](https://doi.org/10.3390/pathogens9100845).
- **Impact on fish health:** BKD is usually chronic but can cause acute mortality, especially in young fish. In sea-phase farms it is usually first noticed as abnormal mortality some time after smolts are transferred to sea, and heavy losses can occur in broodstock and high-density systems [7](https://www.vetinst.no/sykdom-og-agens/bakteriell-nyresyke-bkd). Cumulative mortality can reach 80% in Pacific salmon and 40% in Atlantic salmon, mainly in juveniles of 6–12 months and in pre-spawning adults [3](https://doi.org/10.3390/pathogens9100845).

**Similar Clinical Presentations**

White nodules, kidney swelling, anaemia and skin lesions are not specific to BKD. The following conditions can look similar at necropsy and should be ruled out (see also Differential Diagnosis below):

| Condition | Overlapping findings |
|---|---|
| [[Nephrocalcinosis]] | Calcified deposits seen as white stripes or nodules in the kidney can resemble BKD [22](https://www.vetinst.no/rapporter-og-publikasjoner/rapporter/2025/norwegian-fish-health-report-2024/_/attachment/inline/6b11b72c-ee8f-4529-921f-1a3d85dc419e:2d59843d7c1e34e9200669ae47f2974d8ee51b6a/Fish%20Health%20Report%202024.pdf). |
| [[Francisellosis (Francisella noatunensis)]] | White granulomas in the spleen, kidney and liver [24](https://doi.org/10.1186/1297-9716-42-47). |
| [[Salmonid Rickettsial Septicaemia (SRS)]] | Kidney pathology and haemorrhages [23](https://aquatechcenter.com/disease_challenge/salmon-rickettsial-syndrome-srs/). |
| [[Furunculosis (Aeromonas spp.)]] | Skin lesions and boils [23](https://aquatechcenter.com/disease_challenge/salmon-rickettsial-syndrome-srs/). |
| Infectious haematopoietic necrosis (IHN) | Systemic disease with kidney involvement [6](https://thefishsite.com/disease-guide/bacterial-kidney-disease-bkd). |

### Causes of Bacterial Kidney Disease (Renibacterium salmoninarum)

**Aetiology**

- **Causative agent:** *Renibacterium salmoninarum* (see Overview) [3](https://doi.org/10.3390/pathogens9100845).

- **Transmission Methods:**
    - **Vertical transmission:** The bacterium enters the egg yolk during fertilisation, so infected broodstock pass it to their offspring inside the egg, where surface disinfection cannot reach it [3](https://doi.org/10.3390/pathogens9100845) [7](https://www.vetinst.no/sykdom-og-agens/bakteriell-nyresyke-bkd).
    - **Horizontal transmission:** Spread between fish through water, faeces (an important source in seawater net pens), contaminated feed, cannibalism and feeding of raw infected offal. Spread has been reported between cages and between farms, and wild or escaped fish can act as reservoirs [3](https://doi.org/10.3390/pathogens9100845).
    - **Vectors and equipment:** In Norway, well boats used for delousing and fish transport have been linked to spread between sites in recent years [7](https://www.vetinst.no/sykdom-og-agens/bakteriell-nyresyke-bkd). Sea lice (*Lepeophtheirus salmonis*) are suspected but unconfirmed vectors [3](https://doi.org/10.3390/pathogens9100845).
    - **Survival outside the host:** *R. salmoninarum* is not thought to survive outside the host for more than about a week, which limits long-distance waterborne spread [3](https://doi.org/10.3390/pathogens9100845).

**Risk Factors**

- **Water temperature:** BKD is a cold-water disease, most active at or below 15 °C [3](https://doi.org/10.3390/pathogens9100845).
    - In a classic experiment, juvenile coho salmon and steelhead trout were injected with the bacterium and held at seven temperatures between 3.9 and 20.5 °C. Mortality was highest (78–100%) between 6.7 and 12.2 °C and declined progressively above 12.2 °C. Sockeye salmon, in contrast, showed close to 100% mortality at every temperature from 6.7 to 20.5 °C, reflecting their high susceptibility [15](https://doi.org/10.1139/f78-002).
    - In Chinook salmon held at 8, 12 and 15 °C, mortality, bacterial load in the kidney and shedding of bacteria into the water were all highest at the lowest temperature [3](https://doi.org/10.3390/pathogens9100845).
    - Low temperatures produce a slower, more chronic disease, with bloody ascites and fibrin membranes over the organs [3](https://doi.org/10.3390/pathogens9100845) [7](https://www.vetinst.no/sykdom-og-agens/bakteriell-nyresyke-bkd). This means infected fish can carry and shed the bacterium for long periods without obvious signs.
    - Stress caused by temperature changes is also a risk factor, and field data show more cases in summer and in fish stocked in spring and summer [3](https://doi.org/10.3390/pathogens9100845).
- **Salinity and seawater transfer:** BKD occurs in both freshwater and seawater [3](https://doi.org/10.3390/pathogens9100845).
    - Infection is often acquired in freshwater (through infected eggs or hatchery water), but disease frequently appears after transfer to sea. In Norwegian farms it is usually first noticed as abnormal mortality some time after smolts are put to sea [7](https://www.vetinst.no/sykdom-og-agens/bakteriell-nyresyke-bkd), and carrier fish can develop active disease after sea transfer or at sexual maturation [7](https://www.vetinst.no/sykdom-og-agens/bakteriell-nyresyke-bkd).
    - In freshwater, kidney damage disrupts the fish's ability to regulate salt and water balance (osmoregulation), causing ascites and exophthalmia [7](https://www.vetinst.no/sykdom-og-agens/bakteriell-nyresyke-bkd).
    - The bacterium also transmits in seawater: faeces of infected fish are an important source in net pens, blue mussels near farms filter it from seawater, and marine fish caught around infected sea cages have tested positive [3](https://doi.org/10.3390/pathogens9100845).
    - Controlled studies that isolate salinity as a variable are scarce. In practice, the period around seawater transfer is the highest-risk point, which is why Norway recommends that smolt groups with BKD are not released to sea [7](https://www.vetinst.no/sykdom-og-agens/bakteriell-nyresyke-bkd).
- **Life stage and physiology:** Vulnerability peaks during smoltification and sexual maturation [7](https://www.vetinst.no/sykdom-og-agens/bakteriell-nyresyke-bkd).
- **Species:** Pacific salmon, especially pink, sockeye and Chinook, are more susceptible than Atlantic salmon and rainbow trout [3](https://doi.org/10.3390/pathogens9100845).
- **Husbandry:** Poor water quality, high stocking densities and stress increase susceptibility [1](https://www.adfg.alaska.gov/static/species/disease/pdfs/fishdiseases/bacterial_kidney_disease.pdf) [2](https://www.glfc.org/pubs/SpecialPubs/sp83_2/pdf/chap21.pdf). Losses are heaviest in broodstock and high-density systems [7](https://www.vetinst.no/sykdom-og-agens/bakteriell-nyresyke-bkd).
- **Farm management:** Weak biosecurity, use of unscreened broodstock and lack of routine health monitoring facilitate spread [1](https://www.adfg.alaska.gov/static/species/disease/pdfs/fishdiseases/bacterial_kidney_disease.pdf) [7](https://www.vetinst.no/sykdom-og-agens/bakteriell-nyresyke-bkd).

### Diagnosis

**Diagnostic Methods**

- **Clinical examination:** BKD is difficult to recognise before white nodules develop in the kidney and other organs. Small dead fish can look decomposed and may be wrongly judged unsuitable for examination [7](https://www.vetinst.no/sykdom-og-agens/bakteriell-nyresyke-bkd). Laboratory confirmation is always required. In Norway, an official diagnosis requires clinical findings consistent with BKD plus detection of *R. salmoninarum* by at least two laboratory methods based on different principles [7](https://www.vetinst.no/sykdom-og-agens/bakteriell-nyresyke-bkd).

- **Laboratory Tests:**
    - **Bacterial culture:** Isolation on KDM-2 or SKDM from kidney and other affected organs. Growth takes several weeks (see Overview), so culture is too slow for rapid diagnosis, but it can be more sensitive than PCR in clinically diseased fish [3](https://doi.org/10.3390/pathogens9100845) [7](https://www.vetinst.no/sykdom-og-agens/bakteriell-nyresyke-bkd). Samples should be frozen if they cannot be chilled and shipped quickly [7](https://www.vetinst.no/sykdom-og-agens/bakteriell-nyresyke-bkd).
    - **PCR and qPCR:** Detect bacterial DNA (targets include the *msa*/p57 gene and 16S rRNA) and are used routinely, together with other methods. qPCR is more sensitive and specific than conventional PCR, particularly for subclinical infection [3](https://doi.org/10.3390/pathogens9100845) [7](https://www.vetinst.no/sykdom-og-agens/bakteriell-nyresyke-bkd). Non-lethal qPCR on pooled gill and cloacal swabs detected about 98% of infected fish in a Swedish study, but with more false positives than kidney tests [18](https://doi.org/10.1111/jfd.13614).
    - **Histopathology and immunohistochemistry (IHC):** Granulomatous inflammation and necrosis in kidney, liver and spleen. In fry, the inflammatory reaction can be minimal and bacteria are hard to see on standard staining, so IHC is used to show bacteria in lesions [3](https://doi.org/10.3390/pathogens9100845) [7](https://www.vetinst.no/sykdom-og-agens/bakteriell-nyresyke-bkd).
    - **Fluorescent antibody test (FAT):** Antibody-based staining of kidney smears. Membrane-filtration FAT is more sensitive than smear FAT when bacterial numbers are low [3](https://doi.org/10.3390/pathogens9100845), but FAT has had limited success in detecting the bacterium in fish without clinical signs [6](https://thefishsite.com/disease-guide/bacterial-kidney-disease-bkd).
    - **Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA):** Measures the amount of p57 antigen in kidney tissue [4](https://doi.org/10.3354/dao059125) [18](https://doi.org/10.1111/jfd.13614). See below for how to interpret it.

**Using ELISA in BKD Diagnosis**

ELISA is widely used for broodstock screening and surveillance, but it measures a protein, not living bacteria, and it should not be used on its own to diagnose BKD or to rule it out:

- **What it detects:** The p57 protein released by *R. salmoninarum* [18](https://doi.org/10.1111/jfd.13614). Results are read as optical density (OD). In the Swedish programme, OD below 0.1 is negative, 0.1–0.3 is a low level and above 0.3 is a high level of infection [18](https://doi.org/10.1111/jfd.13614).
- **It can miss infected fish (false negatives):** Kidney ELISA detected only about half (48.8%) of infected fish, compared with about 98% for kidney qPCR [18](https://doi.org/10.1111/jfd.13614). A negative ELISA therefore does not prove a population is free of infection.
- **It can be positive without detectable bacteria:** Because p57 persists in tissue, ELISA can stay positive when DNA is no longer found. For example, after erythromycin or azithromycin treatment, ELISA detected infection in fish that qPCR scored negative [3](https://doi.org/10.3390/pathogens9100845). In a Swedish experiment, all fish with low ELISA values (OD 0.1–0.2) tested negative by qPCR [18](https://doi.org/10.1111/jfd.13614). Low positive readings should be confirmed with another method.
- **Cross-reactions with other bacteria:** Polyclonal antisera against *R. salmoninarum* share a common antigen with *Carnobacterium piscicola* and *Corynebacterium aquaticum*, two other bacteria found in fish [19](https://doi.org/10.1577/1548-8667%281993%29005%3C0172%3ADOACAA%3E2.3.CO%3B2). Polyclonal ELISAs are more sensitive than monoclonal ones [3](https://doi.org/10.3390/pathogens9100845), but this antigen sharing means a positive polyclonal test can occasionally reflect a different bacterium.
- **Poor agreement with other antibody tests:** ELISA and FAT results agree poorly [3](https://doi.org/10.3390/pathogens9100845).
- **Practical recommendation:** Combine ELISA with a DNA-based test (qPCR) and, where possible, culture or histopathology. Confirm positives with lethal kidney sampling before a previously negative farm is placed under restrictions [18](https://doi.org/10.1111/jfd.13614).

**Differential Diagnosis**

- **Distinguishing BKD from other diseases:** BKD must be differentiated from other conditions that cause white nodules, kidney swelling, anaemia or skin lesions, including [[Nephrocalcinosis]] [22](https://www.vetinst.no/rapporter-og-publikasjoner/rapporter/2025/norwegian-fish-health-report-2024/_/attachment/inline/6b11b72c-ee8f-4529-921f-1a3d85dc419e:2d59843d7c1e34e9200669ae47f2974d8ee51b6a/Fish%20Health%20Report%202024.pdf), [[Francisellosis (Francisella noatunensis)|francisellosis]] [24](https://doi.org/10.1186/1297-9716-42-47), [[Salmonid Rickettsial Septicaemia (SRS)|SRS]] and [[Furunculosis (Aeromonas spp.)|furunculosis]] [23](https://aquatechcenter.com/disease_challenge/salmon-rickettsial-syndrome-srs/), and infectious haematopoietic necrosis (IHN) [6](https://thefishsite.com/disease-guide/bacterial-kidney-disease-bkd). *R. salmoninarum* only grows on specialised media and is readily distinguished from other bacteria once isolated [6](https://thefishsite.com/disease-guide/bacterial-kidney-disease-bkd) [7](https://www.vetinst.no/sykdom-og-agens/bakteriell-nyresyke-bkd).

### Treatment and Prevention

**Treatment Options**

- **Current treatments:** No treatment cures BKD or clears the bacterium from a population [7](https://www.vetinst.no/sykdom-og-agens/bakteriell-nyresyke-bkd). Erythromycin, azithromycin and enrofloxacin can reduce disease, but treatment is slow, needs long courses, and resistant strains have been reported [3](https://doi.org/10.3390/pathogens9100845). Injecting adult broodstock with erythromycin before spawning has been used to reduce vertical transmission in Chinook salmon [8](https://doi.org/10.1577/1548-8667%281999%29011%3C0001%3ATTAEOI%3E2.0.CO%3B2). In Norway, treatment is considered relevant only for valuable or endangered broodstock, and broodstock injection is not recommended in commercial farming [7](https://www.vetinst.no/sykdom-og-agens/bakteriell-nyresyke-bkd).

- **Vaccines:** Killed (bacterin) vaccines have shown doubtful efficacy [3](https://doi.org/10.3390/pathogens9100845). A field trial of four vaccines in Atlantic salmon after a natural outbreak gave limited protection [9](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-2761.2010.01151.x). The live *Arthrobacter davidanieli* vaccine Renogen is the only commercial option and offers partial protection. It is currently available only in the USA, Canada and Chile [9](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-2761.2010.01151.x) [10](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10499-023-01290-6). The Norwegian Veterinary Institute considers that no effective vaccine is currently available [7](https://www.vetinst.no/sykdom-og-agens/bakteriell-nyresyke-bkd).

**Preventive Measures**

- **Broodstock control:** Because the bacterium is transmitted inside the egg, keeping it out of broodstock is the most important measure [7](https://www.vetinst.no/sykdom-og-agens/bakteriell-nyresyke-bkd). This includes screening broodstock (combining ELISA and qPCR, see Diagnosis), culling positive fish and their eggs, and intensifying surveillance in spring and summer before broodstock are moved to freshwater [7](https://www.vetinst.no/sykdom-og-agens/bakteriell-nyresyke-bkd) [18](https://doi.org/10.1111/jfd.13614).

- **Types of Disinfection:**
    - **Egg disinfection (iodophors):** Eggs are routinely disinfected with an iodophor solution of 100 ppm available iodine for at least 10 minutes, at pH 6–8 (below pH 6 toxicity to eggs increases) [21](https://woah.org/fileadmin/Home/eng/Health_standards/aahm/2009/1.1.3_DISINFECTION.pdf). This kills bacteria on the egg surface but **does not reliably prevent vertical transmission**, because the bacterium is inside the egg [3](https://doi.org/10.3390/pathogens9100845) [7](https://www.vetinst.no/sykdom-og-agens/bakteriell-nyresyke-bkd).
    - **Water and surface disinfection (chlorine):** *R. salmoninarum* is very sensitive to free chlorine: 99% of bacteria were inactivated within 18 seconds at 0.05 mg/L free chlorine (pH 7, 15 °C). Chlorine worked best at neutral or acidic pH and at lower temperature, and is considered suitable for hatchery use [20](https://doi.org/10.1016/0044-8486%2894%2900361-Q).
    - **Equipment and well boats:** Thorough cleaning followed by disinfection of well boats, nets, tanks and other equipment between sites, since well boats have been linked to recent spread in Norway [7](https://www.vetinst.no/sykdom-og-agens/bakteriell-nyresyke-bkd).
    - **Site disinfection and fallowing:** After depopulation, sites are cleaned, disinfected and fallowed [5](https://food.ec.europa.eu/document/download/e0fa9624-88b7-438a-83a0-294a2622d593_en?filename=sci-com_scah_out36_en.pdf) [14](https://www.mattilsynet.no/fisk-og-akvakultur/fiskesykdommer/listeforing-og-handtering-av-akvatiske-sykdommer).

- **Biosecurity and Farm Management Practices:**
    - Strict biosecurity to prevent introduction, since *R. salmoninarum* is particularly difficult to eradicate once established [5](https://food.ec.europa.eu/document/download/e0fa9624-88b7-438a-83a0-294a2622d593_en?filename=sci-com_scah_out36_en.pdf) [7](https://www.vetinst.no/sykdom-og-agens/bakteriell-nyresyke-bkd) [11](https://doi.org/10.1111/jwas.12966).
    - Sourcing eggs and fish only from BKD-free, screened populations, and not releasing BKD-positive smolt groups to sea [7](https://www.vetinst.no/sykdom-og-agens/bakteriell-nyresyke-bkd).
    - Reducing stocking densities and maintaining good water quality [4](https://doi.org/10.3354/dao059125) [11](https://doi.org/10.1111/jwas.12966).
    - Regular health monitoring and early detection programmes [5](https://food.ec.europa.eu/document/download/e0fa9624-88b7-438a-83a0-294a2622d593_en?filename=sci-com_scah_out36_en.pdf).
    - Slaughter or destruction of infected populations, as no one has yet managed to clear an infected population [7](https://www.vetinst.no/sykdom-og-agens/bakteriell-nyresyke-bkd).

### Case Studies

**Real-World Examples**

- **Notable Outbreaks:**
    - **Scotland (1930s):** First described in wild Atlantic salmon in the rivers Dee and Spey [3](https://doi.org/10.3390/pathogens9100845) [12](https://www.gov.scot/publications/diseases-of-wild-and-farmed-finfish/pages/bacterial-kidney-disease-bkd/).
    - **Japan (1970s):** BKD is believed to have been introduced with imports of coho salmon eggs certified as infection-free [5](https://food.ec.europa.eu/document/download/e0fa9624-88b7-438a-83a0-294a2622d593_en?filename=sci-com_scah_out36_en.pdf).
    - **Sweden (1985–2003):** After the first diagnosis in 1985, BKD was found on 70 farms, spread mainly through transport of live fish [18](https://doi.org/10.1111/jfd.13614).
    - **Chile (2012–2016):** Genome sequencing of 42 isolates revealed four groups, pointing to multiple introductions from different countries [3](https://doi.org/10.3390/pathogens9100845).
    - **Norway (2023–2024):** After years with only 0–3 cases per year, BKD was confirmed at 11 sea sites and one land-based broodstock facility in 2023, and at 8 sites in 2024, in farmed Atlantic salmon and rainbow trout. Genome sequencing showed two independent epidemics caused by different bacterial variants [7](https://www.vetinst.no/sykdom-og-agens/bakteriell-nyresyke-bkd).

- **Response Strategies and Outcomes:**
    - **Scotland (1997):** When BKD was diagnosed in a rainbow trout farm, the farm and a downstream farm were emptied, cleaned, disinfected and restocked after 60 days of fallowing. The disease reappeared the following year [5](https://food.ec.europa.eu/document/download/e0fa9624-88b7-438a-83a0-294a2622d593_en?filename=sci-com_scah_out36_en.pdf).
    - **Sweden:** An eradication programme based on necropsy and culture (from the early 1990s), ELISA (from 1994) and qPCR confirmation (from 2008) reduced new cases from up to 26 per year to between none and a few. Most recent cases are reinfections of recently sanitised farms, and the eradication goal has not yet been reached [18](https://doi.org/10.1111/jfd.13614).
    - **Norway:** Infected sites were placed under movement restrictions, infected populations were slaughtered, and the source of each outbreak was traced. Wild broodstock are considered the main source of recent cases [7](https://www.vetinst.no/sykdom-og-agens/bakteriell-nyresyke-bkd). By 2025 only one commercial salmon site was confirmed [13](https://www.vetinst.no/rapporter-og-publikasjoner/rapporter/2026/fiskehelserapporten-2025/_/attachment/inline/363a7595-e0c3-4ee8-87e6-71ba808b4288:b3c8e9bf1ee7902bdc1ab93334e5219666ceabbd/Fiskehelserapporten%202025.pdf).

- **Lessons Learned:**
    - **Health certificates are not a guarantee.** Subclinical carriers can pass screening, as the introduction of BKD to Japan with certified eggs shows [5](https://food.ec.europa.eu/document/download/e0fa9624-88b7-438a-83a0-294a2622d593_en?filename=sci-com_scah_out36_en.pdf). Testing should combine methods, since a single ELISA can miss about half of infected fish [18](https://doi.org/10.1111/jfd.13614).
    - **Movement of live fish and eggs is the main route of spread.** This was the main factor in Sweden and explains the multiple introductions into Chile [3](https://doi.org/10.3390/pathogens9100845) [18](https://doi.org/10.1111/jfd.13614).
    - **Fallowing alone may not be enough.** Reinfection after cleaning and fallowing in Scotland and Sweden shows that the source of infection (wild fish, water supply, neighbouring farms or equipment) must be identified and removed [5](https://food.ec.europa.eu/document/download/e0fa9624-88b7-438a-83a0-294a2622d593_en?filename=sci-com_scah_out36_en.pdf) [18](https://doi.org/10.1111/jfd.13614).
    - **Freedom can be lost quickly.** Norway's surveillance found no infection in more than 22,000 farmed fish between 2005 and 2011, yet BKD re-emerged at 20 sites in 2023–2024, linked to wild broodstock and to well boats moving between sites [7](https://www.vetinst.no/sykdom-og-agens/bakteriell-nyresyke-bkd).
    - **Egg disinfection does not stop vertical transmission.** Broodstock screening and culling are the key barriers [3](https://doi.org/10.3390/pathogens9100845) [7](https://www.vetinst.no/sykdom-og-agens/bakteriell-nyresyke-bkd).

## Data Insights

### Disease Impact by Country

#### Scotland

- **BKD Incidence in Scotland:**
    - The prevalence of BKD in trout and salmon farms is low [6](https://thefishsite.com/disease-guide/bacterial-kidney-disease-bkd).
    - Between 1990 and 2002, BKD was not recorded in wild salmonids from Scotland, with the last recorded case in wild fish occurring in the 1960s [4](https://doi.org/10.3354/dao059125).

- **Control Measures:**
    - All fish farms holding salmon or trout are inspected at least once a year for BKD [6](https://thefishsite.com/disease-guide/bacterial-kidney-disease-bkd).
    - Farms confirmed as infected are placed under movement restrictions and must enter an eradication programme [6](https://thefishsite.com/disease-guide/bacterial-kidney-disease-bkd).

#### Norway

- **BKD Incidence in Norway:**
    - First detected in 1980, in offspring of wild salmon broodstock. For many years there were only 0–3 cases per year, mostly on the west coast [7](https://www.vetinst.no/sykdom-og-agens/bakteriell-nyresyke-bkd).
    - Surveillance between 2005 and 2011 tested more than 22,000 farmed fish without detecting the bacterium, and surveillance continued until about 2021 [7](https://www.vetinst.no/sykdom-og-agens/bakteriell-nyresyke-bkd).
    - In 2023, BKD was confirmed at 11 sea sites and one land-based broodstock facility in Vestland, Møre og Romsdal and Trøndelag. In 2024, eight sites were affected in Møre og Romsdal and Trøndelag [7](https://www.vetinst.no/sykdom-og-agens/bakteriell-nyresyke-bkd).
    - The situation stabilised in 2025 with only one confirmed case at a commercial salmon site [13](https://www.vetinst.no/rapporter-og-publikasjoner/rapporter/2026/fiskehelserapporten-2025/_/attachment/inline/363a7595-e0c3-4ee8-87e6-71ba808b4288:b3c8e9bf1ee7902bdc1ab93334e5219666ceabbd/Fiskehelserapporten%202025.pdf).
    - The last detection in wild salmon was by PCR in 2019, during health screening of wild-caught broodstock [7](https://www.vetinst.no/sykdom-og-agens/bakteriell-nyresyke-bkd).

- **Control Measures:**
    - BKD is a listed disease in Norway (Category F); any occurrence or suspicion must be reported to the Norwegian Food Safety Authority (Mattilsynet) [7](https://www.vetinst.no/sykdom-og-agens/bakteriell-nyresyke-bkd).
    - Suspicion triggers an immediate ban on fish movements and may lead to mandatory slaughter, site disinfection and a strictly enforced fallowing period [14](https://www.mattilsynet.no/fisk-og-akvakultur/fiskesykdommer/listeforing-og-handtering-av-akvatiske-sykdommer).
    - Broodstock taken from wild anadromous fish must be necropsied and tested for BKD [7](https://www.vetinst.no/sykdom-og-agens/bakteriell-nyresyke-bkd).

#### Sweden

- **BKD Incidence in Sweden:**
    - First diagnosed in 1985, probably introduced with imported frozen fish or eggs. Between 1985 and 2003, 70 farms had clinical BKD or subclinical infection [18](https://doi.org/10.1111/jfd.13614).
    - Index cases have fallen from up to 26 per year to between none and a few per year. Wild whitefish and grayling in a river near infected farms have tested positive [18](https://doi.org/10.1111/jfd.13614).

- **Control Measures:**
    - Mandatory health control samples 30 fish per farm, with ELISA screening and qPCR confirmation. EU-backed eradication guarantees ended in 2021 and were renewed as national measures until 2027 [18](https://doi.org/10.1111/jfd.13614).

## Conclusion

Bacterial Kidney Disease, caused by *Renibacterium salmoninarum*, remains one of the most difficult bacterial diseases of salmonids to control. The bacterium lives inside host cells, is passed from broodstock to offspring inside the egg, causes long-lasting subclinical infections, especially at low water temperatures, and often only becomes visible after seawater transfer or at maturation. Antibiotics and vaccines offer limited help, so prevention depends on screening broodstock with a combination of diagnostic tests, avoiding movements of fish and eggs from untested sources, disinfecting equipment and well boats, and acting quickly when infection is detected. The re-emergence of BKD in Norway after decades of freedom shows that this vigilance cannot be relaxed.
