## Overview

### What is Furunculosis (*Aeromonas salmonicida*)?

Furunculosis is a bacterial disease of fish caused by *Aeromonas salmonicida*, a non-motile, Gram-negative rod of the family *Aeromonadaceae*. It is the oldest known infectious agent linked to fish disease, and the bacterium is found almost worldwide in both freshwater and marine environments. The disease is named after the raised, bloody boils (furuncles) that form in the musculature of larger fish [17](https://www.vetinst.no/sykdom-og-agens/furunkulose) [27](https://doi.org/10.3354/dao03006). Two forms are recognised, depending on the bacterial variant involved [17](https://www.vetinst.no/sykdom-og-agens/furunkulose) [20](https://doi.org/10.3354/dao032049):

- **Classical (typical) furunculosis** is caused by *A. salmonicida* subsp. *salmonicida*, often called "typical" *A. salmonicida*.
- **Atypical furunculosis** is caused by all other variants, grouped together as "atypical" *A. salmonicida*. These include subsp. *achromogenes*, *masoucida* and *smithia*, and a large number of strains that do not belong to any described subspecies.

Subsp. *pectinolytica* also falls outside subsp. *salmonicida*, but it is a mesophilic bacterium isolated from polluted freshwater, lacks the A-layer, and has not been associated with disease in fish [3](https://doi.org/10.3390/md13063791) [26](https://doi.org/10.1093/femsle/fnz074).

**Classical furunculosis** mainly affects salmonids in freshwater and seawater. In Norway, Atlantic salmon (*Salmo salar*), brown trout (*Salmo trutta*, including sea trout) and Arctic char (*Salvelinus alpinus*) are the most susceptible species. Rainbow trout (*Oncorhynchus mykiss*) is considered more resistant in Norway, but the disease is a significant problem in rainbow trout farming in other countries. Lumpfish (*Cyclopterus lumpus*) and turbot (*Scophthalmus maximus*) can also be infected, to a lesser degree. Infected fish can carry the bacterium without clinical signs, and these hidden infections may develop into disease after stressful handling, transport or sorting [17](https://www.vetinst.no/sykdom-og-agens/furunkulose).

**Atypical furunculosis** has a much broader host range. Atypical strains have been isolated from many farmed and wild fish species, both salmonids and non-salmonids, in freshwater, brackish water and marine environments [20](https://doi.org/10.3354/dao032049). Most genetic variants are closely tied to particular host groups, such as Atlantic cod (*Gadus morhua*), wrasse (Labridae), lumpfish, flatfish and cyprinids [26](https://doi.org/10.1093/femsle/fnz074). In Norwegian aquaculture, atypical furunculosis is mainly a disease of cleaner fish used to control salmon lice. Wild-caught wrasse can carry the bacterium without signs of disease, and infection can develop rapidly and cause high mortality after the fish are stocked in sea cages [18](https://www.vetinst.no/rapporter-og-publikasjoner/rapporter/2026/fiskehelserapporten-2025/_/attachment/inline/363a7595-e0c3-4ee8-87e6-71ba808b4288:b3c8e9bf1ee7902bdc1ab93334e5219666ceabbd/Fiskehelserapporten%202025.pdf). In Norway, atypical *A. salmonicida* is also a regular cause of disease in farmed Atlantic halibut (*Hippoglossus hippoglossus*) and has been reported as a problem in spotted wolffish (*Anarhichas minor*) [18](https://www.vetinst.no/rapporter-og-publikasjoner/rapporter/2026/fiskehelserapporten-2025/_/attachment/inline/363a7595-e0c3-4ee8-87e6-71ba808b4288:b3c8e9bf1ee7902bdc1ab93334e5219666ceabbd/Fiskehelserapporten%202025.pdf). Among the A-layer types, type 6 comes mainly from wrasse (55% of isolates) and lumpfish (38%), type 5 almost only from wrasse, type 3 from salmonids (45%) and cod (41%), and type 4 mainly from wolffish [26](https://doi.org/10.1093/femsle/fnz074).

**Classical vs. atypical furunculosis**

The table below summarises the main differences between the two forms [17](https://www.vetinst.no/sykdom-og-agens/furunkulose) [18](https://www.vetinst.no/rapporter-og-publikasjoner/rapporter/2026/fiskehelserapporten-2025/_/attachment/inline/363a7595-e0c3-4ee8-87e6-71ba808b4288:b3c8e9bf1ee7902bdc1ab93334e5219666ceabbd/Fiskehelserapporten%202025.pdf) [20](https://doi.org/10.3354/dao032049) [25](https://doi.org/10.3390/microorganisms12010064) [26](https://doi.org/10.1093/femsle/fnz074) [28](https://doi.org/10.1046/j.1365-2761.2002.00407.x).

|---|---|---|
| **Causative agent** | *A. salmonicida* subsp. *salmonicida* | All other *A. salmonicida* variants: subsp. *achromogenes*, *masoucida*, *smithia* and many unassigned strains |
| **Bacterial characteristics** | Abundant brown, water-soluble pigment on agar; best growth at 20–25 °C; genetically uniform (a single A-layer type) | Usually slower growth, smaller colonies and little or no pigment; biochemically and genetically diverse (more than 20 A-layer types, most of them host-specific) |
| **Main hosts** | Salmonids: Atlantic salmon, brown trout, Arctic char and rainbow trout; occasionally lumpfish and turbot | Salmonids and many non-salmonids, including Atlantic cod, wrasse, lumpfish, Atlantic halibut, spotted wolffish, turbot, wild flatfish, carp and goldfish |
| **Geographical distribution** | Mainly cultivated salmonids in the North Atlantic and North Pacific regions; also wild salmonids in rivers | Freshwater, brackish and marine waters in northern and central Europe, North America, Japan, Australia and South Africa; also detected in Chilean salmon farming |
| **Clinical presentation** | Peracute, acute/subacute and chronic forms; septicaemia with furuncles and skin ulcers in larger fish | In many non-salmonids, mainly superficial skin ulcers; in cod, chronic systemic infection with granulomas containing bacterial colonies; in lumpfish, bacterial microcolonies in internal organs; in salmonids, can resemble classical disease |

| **Legal status in Norway** | Notifiable (listed disease, category F) | Not notifiable |

Furunculosis remains a major health problem for salmonid aquaculture worldwide, causing significant economic losses and promoting intensive antibiotic use. Effective oil-adjuvanted vaccines are commercially available, but outbreaks still occur [1](https://doi.org/10.1111/1751-7915.12091). In Norway, vaccination and biosecurity measures introduced in the early 1990s have kept classical furunculosis under good control (see Case Studies) [17](https://www.vetinst.no/sykdom-og-agens/furunkulose). However, *A. salmonicida* subsp. *salmonicida* has been shown to become more pathogenic at temperatures only slightly above current summer temperatures, and it is uncertain whether vaccine protection will be equally effective in a warmer sea, so more outbreaks under climate change cannot be ruled out [18](https://www.vetinst.no/rapporter-og-publikasjoner/rapporter/2026/fiskehelserapporten-2025/_/attachment/inline/363a7595-e0c3-4ee8-87e6-71ba808b4288:b3c8e9bf1ee7902bdc1ab93334e5219666ceabbd/Fiskehelserapporten%202025.pdf).

## Clinical Signs of Furunculosis (*Aeromonas salmonicida*)

### Common Signs

Clinical signs differ between the two forms of the disease and between host species, so they are described separately below.

**Classical furunculosis**

- **Physical Signs:**
    - **Furuncles:** Raised, bloody boils under the skin or deep in the musculature, containing a reddish-brown mass of bacteria, blood cells and broken-down muscle tissue. They are most common in larger fish, can open into skin ulcers, and are usually absent in the peracute form [17](https://www.vetinst.no/sykdom-og-agens/furunkulose) [19](https://snl.no/furunkulose_-_fisk).
    - **Skin haemorrhages:** Haemorrhages on the fins and the ventral part of the body [5](https://doi.org/10.3390/biology14101330).
    - **Exophthalmia:** Protruding eyes, seen both in hatchery outbreaks and in experimental infection [5](https://doi.org/10.3390/biology14101330) [17](https://www.vetinst.no/sykdom-og-agens/furunkulose).
    - **Darkening of the skin:** Particularly in hatchery outbreaks [17](https://www.vetinst.no/sykdom-og-agens/furunkulose).
    - **Fin rot and anal prolapse:** Fin rot can occur in the acute/subacute form, and anal prolapse is typical of the chronic form [19](https://snl.no/furunkulose_-_fisk).
- **Internal Findings:**
    - **Spleen:** Swollen and dark [17](https://www.vetinst.no/sykdom-og-agens/furunkulose).
    - **Haemorrhages:** Petechial haemorrhages on the peritoneum and internal organs, and ascites (fluid in the abdominal cavity) [17](https://www.vetinst.no/sykdom-og-agens/furunkulose).
    - **Liver and kidney:** Focal necrosis in the liver, and oedema, congestion and petechial haemorrhages in the kidney [5](https://doi.org/10.3390/biology14101330).
    - **Intestine:** Bloody discolouration of the intestine in the chronic form [19](https://snl.no/furunkulose_-_fisk).
- **Behavioural Signs:**
    - **Loss of appetite and abnormal swimming:** Anorexia and swimming disorders are typical signs [5](https://doi.org/10.3390/biology14101330).
    - **Reduced activity:** In experimentally infected rainbow trout, fish first became less active and stayed at the bottom of the tank or near the water inflow. Later, they lost coordination and swam erratically, and fish with severe skin lesions rubbed their bodies against the tank walls ("flashing") [5](https://doi.org/10.3390/biology14101330).
    - **Rapid breathing:** A very high respiration rate is common in hatchery outbreaks [17](https://www.vetinst.no/sykdom-og-agens/furunkulose).

**Progression of Clinical Signs**

All four forms of classical furunculosis can occur in the same outbreak [19](https://snl.no/furunkulose_-_fisk).

- **Peracute form:** High mortality with few or no specific signs, apart from small internal haemorrhages and an enlarged spleen. In hatcheries, fish turn dark, breathe rapidly, show moderate exophthalmia, and can die quickly with no other external signs [17](https://www.vetinst.no/sykdom-og-agens/furunkulose) [19](https://snl.no/furunkulose_-_fisk).
- **Acute/subacute form:** A septicaemia with a somewhat longer course and lower mortality than the peracute form, seen mainly in larger fish. Furuncles, fin rot, skin ulcers and necrosis in internal organs can occur, and fish die within a few days [19](https://snl.no/furunkulose_-_fisk).
- **Chronic form:** Also called the intestinal form. It mainly shows as bloody discolouration of the intestine and anal prolapse, with usually low mortality [19](https://snl.no/furunkulose_-_fisk).
- **Latent infection (carrier state):** Fish show no mortality or visible changes, but *A. salmonicida* can be isolated from their internal organs, and the share of carriers can be high. Latent infections can develop into disease after stressful handling, transport or sorting [17](https://www.vetinst.no/sykdom-og-agens/furunkulose) [19](https://snl.no/furunkulose_-_fisk).

**Atypical furunculosis**

The clinical picture of atypical furunculosis varies with the host species and the bacterial strain [20](https://doi.org/10.3354/dao032049).

- **Non-salmonid fish in general:** Infection mainly shows as skin ulcers. Internal organs are infrequently invaded, and muscle abscesses are only occasionally seen. Well-known examples are carp erythrodermatitis, goldfish ulcer disease and ulcer disease of flounder [19](https://snl.no/furunkulose_-_fisk) [20](https://doi.org/10.3354/dao032049).
- **Atlantic salmon:** In the first outbreaks described in Chilean freshwater farms, fish showed superficial ulcers and a pale liver with or without petechial haemorrhages [31](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-2761.2010.01142.x). In more recent Chilean outbreaks, fish showed haemorrhages around the eyes, at the fin bases and on the abdomen, furuncles, skin ulcers, petechial haemorrhages in internal organs, enlarged spleen and kidney, and haemorrhagic enteritis, a picture that can resemble classical furunculosis [25](https://doi.org/10.3390/microorganisms12010064).
- **Atlantic cod:** Skin ulcers and widespread granulomas in internal organs [28](https://doi.org/10.1046/j.1365-2761.2002.00407.x). White nodules in the spleen and kidney make the disease look very similar to francisellosis at necropsy (see Differential Diagnosis) [18](https://www.vetinst.no/rapporter-og-publikasjoner/rapporter/2026/fiskehelserapporten-2025/_/attachment/inline/363a7595-e0c3-4ee8-87e6-71ba808b4288:b3c8e9bf1ee7902bdc1ab93334e5219666ceabbd/Fiskehelserapporten%202025.pdf) [21](https://doi.org/10.1186/1297-9716-42-47).
- **Ballan wrasse:** In experimental bath challenge, juvenile wrasse developed a systemic infection with enlarged liver and spleen, granulomatous hepatitis with bacterial colonies, and inflammation of the heart lining (endocarditis) [30](https://doi.org/10.1111/jfd.13312).
- **Lumpfish:** Atypical *A. salmonicida* is frequently associated with disease and mortality in farmed lumpfish [29](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.fsi.2017.03.040). Microcolonies of bacteria can be seen in the heart [18](https://www.vetinst.no/rapporter-og-publikasjoner/rapporter/2026/fiskehelserapporten-2025/_/attachment/inline/363a7595-e0c3-4ee8-87e6-71ba808b4288:b3c8e9bf1ee7902bdc1ab93334e5219666ceabbd/Fiskehelserapporten%202025.pdf).
- **Behavioural Signs:** In Chilean outbreaks in Atlantic salmon, affected fish showed reduced appetite and increased lethargy [25](https://doi.org/10.3390/microorganisms12010064).

### Causes of Furunculosis (*Aeromonas salmonicida*)

**Aetiology**

- **Classical furunculosis:** Caused by *A. salmonicida* subsp. *salmonicida*, a non-motile rod with rounded ends. It produces abundant brown, water-soluble pigment on media containing tyrosine or phenylalanine, grows best at 20–25 °C [17](https://www.vetinst.no/sykdom-og-agens/furunkulose), and can usually be isolated in pure culture from dead fish [19](https://snl.no/furunkulose_-_fisk). Its genome is highly conserved worldwide and over time [1](https://doi.org/10.1111/1751-7915.12091).
- **Atypical furunculosis:** Caused by all other *A. salmonicida* variants. These usually grow more slowly, form smaller colonies and produce little or no pigment [17](https://www.vetinst.no/sykdom-og-agens/furunkulose). They are a very heterogeneous group in biochemical characteristics, growth conditions and production of extracellular proteases [20](https://doi.org/10.3354/dao032049), and comprise more than 20 genetic types (A-layer types), most of them associated with particular host species [26](https://doi.org/10.1093/femsle/fnz074).
- **Key Virulence Factors:**
    - **A-layer:** A crystalline surface layer made of a 49 kDa protein (VapA, encoded by the *vapA* gene) and anchored to the cell by lipopolysaccharide (LPS). Together with the LPS O-antigen, it protects the bacterium from being killed by serum, and it is an important barrier against phagocytosis. Typical and atypical strains share the A-layer, except subsp. *pectinolytica* [3](https://doi.org/10.3390/md13063791).
    - **Type III secretion system (T3SS):** Injects effector proteins into host cells and induces a temporary immunosuppression that gives the bacterium free access to host tissues. The later stages of disease, with circulatory failure and mortality, are probably dominated by other virulence factors such as a serine protease and the GCAT lipase [1](https://doi.org/10.1111/1751-7915.12091).
    - **Cold shock proteins:** CspB and CspD contribute to virulence by regulating virulence genes on the chromosome and on the large pASal virulence plasmid [4](https://doi.org/10.1128/iai.00011-24).
    - **Atypical strains:** Some atypical strains also have an A-layer, but other virulence mechanisms have been described, such as an extracellular metalloprotease and a different iron uptake system [20](https://doi.org/10.3354/dao032049). In lumpfish, a highly virulent atypical isolate expressed the A-layer while a less virulent isolate did not [29](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.fsi.2017.03.040).
- **Transmission Methods:**
    - **Horizontal transmission:** Fish-to-fish transmission is the main route [17](https://www.vetinst.no/sykdom-og-agens/furunkulose). The bacterium can enter through the skin, gills and intestine, most likely through injuries to the epithelial barrier [1](https://doi.org/10.1111/1751-7915.12091). Genomic analysis of outbreak isolates from several farms showed that geographically separated farms can share a single epidemiological unit [10](https://doi.org/10.3390/pathogens13100908).
    - **Movement of live fish:** Classical furunculosis was reintroduced to Norway in 1985 with latently infected smolts from Scotland and later spread to more than 70 Norwegian rivers [17](https://www.vetinst.no/sykdom-og-agens/furunkulose). Transport of live fish has probably also spread some atypical genetic types almost worldwide, for example types associated with carp and goldfish [26](https://doi.org/10.1093/femsle/fnz074).
    - **Environmental reservoir:** The bacterium can survive longer, and remain pathogenic, in sediment containing faeces and food waste [1](https://doi.org/10.1111/1751-7915.12091).
    - **Cleaner fish:** Wild-caught wrasse, including wrasse imported from Sweden, have unknown health status and can be healthy carriers of atypical *A. salmonicida*, which makes them a major biosecurity risk [18](https://www.vetinst.no/rapporter-og-publikasjoner/rapporter/2026/fiskehelserapporten-2025/_/attachment/inline/363a7595-e0c3-4ee8-87e6-71ba808b4288:b3c8e9bf1ee7902bdc1ab93334e5219666ceabbd/Fiskehelserapporten%202025.pdf).

**Risk Factors**

- **Classical furunculosis:**
    - **Temperature:** The disease occurs mainly at water temperatures of about 10 °C or higher, while below about 7–8 °C infection tends to remain latent [19](https://snl.no/furunkulose_-_fisk). Outbreaks are most common in summer and autumn, when water temperature is highest, and in wild salmonids during periods of high water temperature and low river flow [17](https://www.vetinst.no/sykdom-og-agens/furunkulose). Furunculosis is expected to become more important in a warmer climate [18](https://www.vetinst.no/rapporter-og-publikasjoner/rapporter/2026/fiskehelserapporten-2025/_/attachment/inline/363a7595-e0c3-4ee8-87e6-71ba808b4288:b3c8e9bf1ee7902bdc1ab93334e5219666ceabbd/Fiskehelserapporten%202025.pdf).
    - **Stress and handling:** Latent infections can develop into disease after stressful handling, transport or sorting [17](https://www.vetinst.no/sykdom-og-agens/furunkulose). Stress that damages the mucus layer or skin is probably needed for infection to establish [1](https://doi.org/10.1111/1751-7915.12091).
    - **Production system:** In Norway, outbreaks have mainly been linked to sea-cage farming and smolt facilities that use seawater, although outbreaks have also occurred in pure freshwater [17](https://www.vetinst.no/sykdom-og-agens/furunkulose).
- **Atypical furunculosis:**
    - **Temperature:** In wild cod held at different temperatures for a year, atypical *A. salmonicida* was isolated from fish kept at 7 °C or higher [28](https://doi.org/10.1046/j.1365-2761.2002.00407.x).
    - **Mixing and handling of cleaner fish:** Long transports are very stressful, and disease can flare up when fish of different origins are mixed in a new, stressful environment. Cleaner fish tolerate crowding, netting and pumping worse than salmon [18](https://www.vetinst.no/rapporter-og-publikasjoner/rapporter/2026/fiskehelserapporten-2025/_/attachment/inline/363a7595-e0c3-4ee8-87e6-71ba808b4288:b3c8e9bf1ee7902bdc1ab93334e5219666ceabbd/Fiskehelserapporten%202025.pdf).

### Diagnosis

**Diagnostic Methods**

- **Clinical Examination:** A presumptive diagnosis of furunculosis can be made from characteristic gross pathology in salmonids, particularly the combination of furuncles, fin-base haemorrhages, exophthalmia, bloody vent discharge, and splenomegaly. However, peracute and acute presentations may lack furuncles entirely, and clinical signs overlap significantly with those of other bacterial septicaemias. Isolation of the causative agent is required for definitive diagnosis, while clinical and histopathological findings provide important supporting evidence for the diagnosis [17](https://www.vetinst.no/sykdom-og-agens/furunkulose).
- **Laboratory Tests:**
    - **Bacterial Culture:** *A. salmonicida* grows on standard media such as blood agar, typically incubated at 22 °C for 2–4 days [26](https://doi.org/10.1093/femsle/fnz074). All pathogenic variants are non-motile. Typical subsp. *salmonicida* produces abundant brown, water-soluble pigment, while atypical variants usually grow more slowly, form smaller colonies and produce little or no pigment [17](https://www.vetinst.no/sykdom-og-agens/furunkulose).
    - **PCR-Based Methods:**
        - **Detection:** Real-time PCR (qPCR) targeting the *vapA* gene, which encodes the A-layer protein, is one of the most widely used assays for *A. salmonicida*. It is used to confirm both typical and atypical strains [2](https://doi.org/10.3389/fcimb.2025.1508135) [25](https://doi.org/10.3390/microorganisms12010064).
        - **Sensitivity, bacterial load and carriers:** qPCR is more sensitive than culture. In Danish rainbow trout farms, a qPCR assay detected *A. salmonicida* in 65% of fish, against 30% by culture, and no qPCR-negative fish was culture-positive. The assay measured bacterial load in each organ, and it detected the bacterium in freshwater fish without signs of furunculosis, indicating possible carrier fish [50](https://doi.org/10.1111/jfd.12505).
        - **Multiplex PCR:** A multiplex PCR detects *A. salmonicida* subsp. *salmonicida*, *Flavobacterium psychrophilum* (bacterial cold-water disease) and *Yersinia ruckeri* (enteric redmouth disease) in a single reaction. Its detection limit for *A. salmonicida* was 30 CFU in pure culture and 250 CFU in liver homogenate [6](https://doi.org/10.4142/jvs.2010.11.3.235).
        - **Limitations in Chile:** A *vapA*-absent strain emerging in Chilean salmon farms does not amplify in *vapA* PCR. Adding a second marker, the *fstA* gene, detects this strain, and a two-marker *vapA*/*fstA* qPCR has been used in routine diagnostics since 2024 [2](https://doi.org/10.3389/fcimb.2025.1508135). *Aeromonas piscicola*, which causes furunculosis-like disease, is negative for both markers (see Differential Diagnosis) [24](https://doi.org/10.3390/pathogens15040402).
        - **Typing:** Sequence variation in the *vapA* gene allows unambiguous subtyping of *A. salmonicida* (A-layer typing). The method has been applied to 675 isolates from 50 fish species in 26 countries, and it is useful as an epidemiological marker [26](https://doi.org/10.1093/femsle/fnz074).
    - **Serological Methods:** Serological assays and immunofluorescence can confirm that an isolate belongs to *A. salmonicida* [31](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-2761.2010.01142.x). ELISA can also measure antibody levels in vaccinated fish, for example to check whether vaccination induces antibodies against newly emerging strains [2](https://doi.org/10.3389/fcimb.2025.1508135).
    - **Histopathology:** Histological sections from muscle, liver, spleen, heart, and kidney reveal necrosis and bacterial aggregates. Muscle tissue around furuncles shows extensive myonecrosis with few inflammatory cells, reflecting the immunoevasive properties of the A-layer. Hepatic and renal necrosis with bacteria in sinusoids and tubular lumina are consistent acute systemic findings, with significant organ dysfunction and persistent pathological changes documented even after the resolution of peak bacteraemia [5](https://doi.org/10.3390/biology14101330).

**Differential Diagnosis**

- **Classical furunculosis in salmonids:** Furunculosis shares many signs with other bacterial diseases, such as dark skin, exophthalmia, fin-base haemorrhages, skin ulcers, an enlarged spleen and septicaemia. Diagnosis therefore relies on culture and identification of the bacterium, supported by PCR [6](https://doi.org/10.4142/jvs.2010.11.3.235) [17](https://www.vetinst.no/sykdom-og-agens/furunkulose) [18](https://www.vetinst.no/rapporter-og-publikasjoner/rapporter/2026/fiskehelserapporten-2025/_/attachment/inline/363a7595-e0c3-4ee8-87e6-71ba808b4288:b3c8e9bf1ee7902bdc1ab93334e5219666ceabbd/Fiskehelserapporten%202025.pdf) [24](https://doi.org/10.3390/pathogens15040402) [51](https://doi.org/10.3390/ani11010125) [52](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13567-015-0238-4) [53](https://doi.org/10.3390/pathogens15010087).

| Winter ulcer ([[Moritella Viscosa]], see also [[Winter Wounds]]) | Skin ulcers; *M. viscosa* can also infect internal organs, even without ulcers | Mainly an autumn and winter problem, often after handling that has damaged the skin, while furunculosis outbreaks peak in summer and autumn |
| [[Tenacibaculosis (Tenacibaculum spp)]] | Skin ulcers | Ulcers mainly on the head and fins; in Norwegian salmonids almost exclusively a skin infection |
| *Aeromonas piscicola* | Furunculosis-like disease in Chilean Atlantic salmon, with ulcerative, haemorrhagic skin lesions, congestion and haemorrhage of internal organs, and septicaemia | Motile and does not produce brown pigment; negative in *vapA* and *fstA* qPCR; 16S rRNA sequencing cannot separate it from *A. salmonicida*, so identification needs whole-genome sequencing or phylogenetic analysis |

- **Atypical furunculosis in cod and cleaner fish:** In cleaner fish, atypical furunculosis is typically a chronic infection with boils, ulcers and granulomas containing bacterial microcolonies in internal organs. Several other bacteria are common causes of disease outbreaks in wrasse and lumpfish and can give similar ulcers or septicaemia. In cod, mixed infections of atypical *A. salmonicida* with *Francisella noatunensis* and/or *V. anguillarum* are relatively common, so finding one pathogen does not rule out the others [18](https://www.vetinst.no/rapporter-og-publikasjoner/rapporter/2026/fiskehelserapporten-2025/_/attachment/inline/363a7595-e0c3-4ee8-87e6-71ba808b4288:b3c8e9bf1ee7902bdc1ab93334e5219666ceabbd/Fiskehelserapporten%202025.pdf) [21](https://doi.org/10.1186/1297-9716-42-47).

| Disease | Key overlapping signs | How to distinguish |
|---|---|---|
| [[Francisellosis (Francisella noatunensis)]] (cod) | White nodules (granulomas) in internal organs, especially spleen and kidney; the two diseases look very similar at necropsy | Histology: in atypical furunculosis the bacteria form colonies in the centre of the granulomas, while in francisellosis they lie inside the cells; *F. noatunensis* needs cysteine-rich culture media |
| [[Vibriosis]] (*V. anguillarum*; cleaner fish, cod) | Ulcers, fin rot, skin haemorrhages and haemorrhages in internal organs | Culture and identification of the isolate |
| [[Pasteurellosis]] (*Phocoenobacter atlanticus* subsp. *cyclopteri*; lumpfish) | Bacterial sepsis with white-spot skin lesions, tail fin rot, ascites, and haemorrhages in the gills and at fin bases | Culture and identification of the isolate |
| *Pseudomonas anguilliseptica* (lumpfish) | Haemorrhagic septicaemia | Culture and identification of the isolate |
| Ulcer-associated bacteria ([[Moritella Viscosa]], [[Tenacibaculosis (Tenacibaculum spp)]]; cleaner fish) | Ulcers and tail fin rot | Culture and identification; *M. viscosa* mainly at lower sea temperatures |

### Treatment and Prevention

**Treatment Options**

- **Current Treatments:**
    - **Antibiotics:** Antibacterial treatment is given in medicated feed. In Norway, florfenicol is used almost exclusively, and oxolinic acid made up less than 0.5% of use in 2025. Most prescriptions now go to farmed marine fish, mainly Atlantic halibut, and atypical furunculosis is the most frequently recorded diagnosis in these species [18](https://www.vetinst.no/rapporter-og-publikasjoner/rapporter/2026/fiskehelserapporten-2025/_/attachment/inline/363a7595-e0c3-4ee8-87e6-71ba808b4288:b3c8e9bf1ee7902bdc1ab93334e5219666ceabbd/Fiskehelserapporten%202025.pdf). In Chile, florfenicol and oxytetracycline account for nearly all antibiotic use in salmon farming [11](https://doi.org/10.3389/fmicb.2018.01284) [16](https://www.sernapesca.cl/app/uploads/2026/03/Informe-de-uso-de-antimicrobianos-ano-2025.pdf).
    - **Antimicrobial resistance:** Resistance in *A. salmonicida* is mostly carried on plasmids. The conjugative multiresistance plasmid pSN254b is the dominant resistance plasmid in Canadian isolates, and variants of the pAsa4 plasmid carry resistance genes against chloramphenicol, sulfonamides and tetracyclines, with a mosaic structure shaped by mobile genetic elements [7](https://doi.org/10.1038/srep35617) [8](https://doi.org/10.7717/peerj.2595).
    - **Selection pressure:** Antibiotic use in fish farms increases the occurrence and abundance of multiresistant *Aeromonas* strains in both fish and the surrounding water [9](https://doi.org/10.3390/microorganisms9061201).
    - **Atypical furunculosis:** Treatment in lumpfish remains challenging. In a challenge study, medicated feed with florfenicol, oxolinic acid or flumequine treated vibriosis effectively, but none of the three agents treated atypical furunculosis effectively. Florfenicol at 20 mg/kg/day looked promising at first, but mortality rose rapidly 14 days after medication ended [39](https://doi.org/10.1111/jfd.13545). In practice, treating sick cleaner fish in sea cages is not considered feasible [18](https://www.vetinst.no/rapporter-og-publikasjoner/rapporter/2026/fiskehelserapporten-2025/_/attachment/inline/363a7595-e0c3-4ee8-87e6-71ba808b4288:b3c8e9bf1ee7902bdc1ab93334e5219666ceabbd/Fiskehelserapporten%202025.pdf). In farmed halibut, by contrast, florfenicol treatment of atypical *A. salmonicida* infection has been reported to work well [38](https://www.vetinst.no/rapporter-og-publikasjoner/rapporter/2025/fiskehelserapporten-2024).

**Preventive Measures**

- **Vaccines:**
    - **Commercial Vaccines:** Vaccines against furunculosis were introduced in Norway in the early 1990s [17](https://www.vetinst.no/sykdom-og-agens/furunkulose) [18](https://www.vetinst.no/rapporter-og-publikasjoner/rapporter/2026/fiskehelserapporten-2025/_/attachment/inline/363a7595-e0c3-4ee8-87e6-71ba808b4288:b3c8e9bf1ee7902bdc1ab93334e5219666ceabbd/Fiskehelserapporten%202025.pdf). Today they are part of multivalent injection vaccines for salmon and rainbow trout that also protect against cold-water vibriosis, vibriosis and, in some vaccines, winter ulcer, IPN, ISA or PD [18](https://www.vetinst.no/rapporter-og-publikasjoner/rapporter/2026/fiskehelserapporten-2025/_/attachment/inline/363a7595-e0c3-4ee8-87e6-71ba808b4288:b3c8e9bf1ee7902bdc1ab93334e5219666ceabbd/Fiskehelserapporten%202025.pdf). A government-industry initiative to vaccinate against classical furunculosis, together with high-quality vaccines, was a key reason for the large fall in antibacterial use in Norway (see Case Studies) [32](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-2109.2010.02726.x).
    - **Vaccine limitations:** Vaccinated fish showed a low antibody response against the *vapA*-absent strain that has emerged in Chilean salmon farms. The study did not test whether current vaccines protect against this strain [2](https://doi.org/10.3389/fcimb.2025.1508135). Oil-adjuvanted vaccines can also cause side effects such as granulomatous reactions and adhesions in the abdominal cavity, pigmentation and spinal deformities. In experimental studies in rainbow trout, alternative adjuvants (CpG oligodeoxynucleotides and the liposomal CAF01 system) reduced these side effects while giving protection comparable to mineral oil [13](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-017-06324-7).
    - **Vaccination against atypical furunculosis:** Atypical isolates are genetically diverse, and most A-layer (*vapA*) types are linked to particular host groups, so protection depends on matching the strains in each host species [26](https://doi.org/10.1093/femsle/fnz074). In farmed salmon, vaccination against subsp. *salmonicida* usually also protects against atypical variants [18](https://www.vetinst.no/rapporter-og-publikasjoner/rapporter/2026/fiskehelserapporten-2025/_/attachment/inline/363a7595-e0c3-4ee8-87e6-71ba808b4288:b3c8e9bf1ee7902bdc1ab93334e5219666ceabbd/Fiskehelserapporten%202025.pdf). In lumpfish, experimental vaccines containing a highly virulent atypical isolate gave 73% (monovalent) and 60% (trivalent) relative percent survival after intraperitoneal challenge [29](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.fsi.2017.03.040). A manufacturer's vaccination manual for lumpfish lists two *A. salmonicida* variants (types V and VI) for injection vaccination and states that an oil adjuvant is needed for long-lasting protection against atypical furunculosis [23](https://www.vaxxinova.no/content/uploads/2020/01/Vaxx_Vaksinemanual_Rognkjeks.pdf). In the field, however, few vaccines are available for farmed cleaner fish, vaccines with better effect are requested, and no effective vaccine against atypical furunculosis in cleaner fish in sea cages has been developed. Fish health personnel also report a lack of effective vaccines for farmed cod [18](https://www.vetinst.no/rapporter-og-publikasjoner/rapporter/2026/fiskehelserapporten-2025/_/attachment/inline/363a7595-e0c3-4ee8-87e6-71ba808b4288:b3c8e9bf1ee7902bdc1ab93334e5219666ceabbd/Fiskehelserapporten%202025.pdf).
    - **Autogenous vaccines:** Where no authorised vaccine exists, or the available ones do not work well enough, farm-specific (autogenous) vaccines can be used after an exemption is granted. In Norway in 2025, approved autogenous vaccines with *A. salmonicida* components included vaccines for cod, lumpfish and turbot [18](https://www.vetinst.no/rapporter-og-publikasjoner/rapporter/2026/fiskehelserapporten-2025/_/attachment/inline/363a7595-e0c3-4ee8-87e6-71ba808b4288:b3c8e9bf1ee7902bdc1ab93334e5219666ceabbd/Fiskehelserapporten%202025.pdf). Genomic analysis showing that outbreak isolates from several farms belong to a single epidemiological unit supports using a shared autogenous vaccine for such farm clusters [10](https://doi.org/10.3390/pathogens13100908).

- **Biosecurity Protocols:**
    - **Screening for carriers:** qPCR can detect *A. salmonicida* in fish without clinical signs, which may be carriers [50](https://doi.org/10.1111/jfd.12505). Screening that relies on *vapA* PCR alone can miss the *vapA*-absent strain found in Chile, while a two-marker *vapA*/*fstA* qPCR detects it [2](https://doi.org/10.3389/fcimb.2025.1508135).
    - **All-in/all-out production:** All-in/all-out production with mandatory fallowing periods between year classes, and zoning of marine sites to limit horizontal spread of infection [32](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-2109.2010.02726.x).

- **Farm Management Practices:**
    - **Stress reduction:** Minimising stressful handling, transport and sorting, which can turn latent infections into disease [17](https://www.vetinst.no/sykdom-og-agens/furunkulose).

### Case Studies

**Real-World Examples**

- **Notable Outbreaks:**
    - **Norway (1985 to early 1990s), the pre-vaccine epidemic:** Classical furunculosis was reintroduced to Norway in 1985 with latently infected smolts from Scotland. From the late 1980s until 1992, outbreaks were frequent in farms along the coast of southern and central Norway, north to Troms, and the disease spread to more than 70 rivers. Biosecurity measures and a vaccination programme in the early 1990s made the disease largely disappear [17](https://www.vetinst.no/sykdom-og-agens/furunkulose). Antibacterial use in Norwegian fish farming fell from about 48 tonnes in 1987 to about 1 tonne per year, helped by vaccination, all-in/all-out production with fallowing, and zoning of marine sites [32](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-2109.2010.02726.x). In 2025, total use was 498 kg, despite a large increase in production [18](https://www.vetinst.no/rapporter-og-publikasjoner/rapporter/2026/fiskehelserapporten-2025/_/attachment/inline/363a7595-e0c3-4ee8-87e6-71ba808b4288:b3c8e9bf1ee7902bdc1ab93334e5219666ceabbd/Fiskehelserapporten%202025.pdf).
    - **Chile (ongoing), furunculosis in the world's second-largest salmon producer:** Chile has been the second-largest salmonid producer for several years, and furunculosis in Atlantic salmon has increased in recent years. Disease in freshwater has become more common since 2022, especially in recirculating systems. Routine *vapA* PCR failed in some of these cases. This led to the discovery of a new *vapA*-absent *A. salmonicida* strain from a different lineage, with altered outer membrane proteins and lipopolysaccharide. The strain accounts for a rising proportion of cases, appears to cause less virulent disease, and vaccinated fish showed a low antibody response against it [2](https://doi.org/10.3389/fcimb.2025.1508135). Re-emergent atypical *A. salmonicida* has also been sequenced from Chilean Atlantic salmon outbreaks [25](https://doi.org/10.3390/microorganisms12010064). Other furunculosis-like cases that tested negative for *A. salmonicida* turned out to be *Aeromonas piscicola*. It caused disease of comparable virulence in challenge trials, and the current vaccine induced only a modest IgM response against it in the field [24](https://doi.org/10.3390/pathogens15040402). Antibiotic use per tonne of harvested salmon has fallen since the mid-2010s, but total use has not, and most of it targets [[Salmonid Rickettsial Septicaemia (SRS)|SRS]] rather than furunculosis (figures under Data Insights) [11](https://doi.org/10.3389/fmicb.2018.01284) [16](https://www.sernapesca.cl/app/uploads/2026/03/Informe-de-uso-de-antimicrobianos-ano-2025.pdf).

- **Lessons Learned:** Norway shows that vaccination, combined with biosecurity measures such as fallowing and zoning, can bring a major bacterial disease under control and cut antibiotic use drastically [17](https://www.vetinst.no/sykdom-og-agens/furunkulose) [32](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-2109.2010.02726.x). In Chile, a new *vapA*-absent strain escaped routine *vapA* PCR and induced a low antibody response in vaccinated fish, which shows the need to monitor circulating strains and update diagnostic targets [2](https://doi.org/10.3389/fcimb.2025.1508135). Together with re-emergent atypical lineages and *A. piscicola*, it suggests that furunculosis in Chile is caused by a group of related agents, each needing its own diagnostic targets [24](https://doi.org/10.3390/pathogens15040402) [25](https://doi.org/10.3390/microorganisms12010064).

## Data Insights

### Disease Impact by Country

#### Chile

- **Furunculosis Incidence:**
    - In SERNAPESCA's passive surveillance of laboratory diagnoses, typical *A. salmonicida* appeared in 284 positive reports from freshwater farms and 238 from marine farms in 2025. In freshwater it was the fifth most frequently detected agent, after piscine orthoreovirus (PRV, 2,055 reports), *Flavobacterium psychrophilum* (946), infectious pancreatic necrosis virus (IPNV, 855) and *Flavobacterium columnare* (297). Freshwater reports came mainly from Los Lagos (154), Magallanes (76) and Aysén (42). Marine reports came from Aysén (103), Los Lagos (82) and Magallanes (53). "Atypical *A. salmonicida*" added 33 freshwater and 31 marine positive reports, from Los Lagos, Aysén and Magallanes [15](https://www.sernapesca.cl/app/uploads/2026/03/Informe-Situacion-Sanitaria-Salmonicultura-Ano-2025.pdf).
    - These counts depend on the laboratory method. A *vapA*-absent *A. salmonicida* strain, first found in freshwater outbreaks in 2022, is missed by PCR assays that target only *vapA*. In one diagnostic laboratory it made up 88% of *A. salmonicida* detections in tissue samples in the first half of 2024 [2](https://doi.org/10.3389/fcimb.2025.1508135). Some *A. salmonicida*-like isolates from freshwater outbreaks since 2022 were negative for both *vapA* and *fstA* and were identified as *Aeromonas piscicola* by whole-genome sequencing [24](https://doi.org/10.3390/pathogens15040402). Official counts may therefore underestimate furunculosis and furunculosis-like disease.

- **Economic Impact:**
    - Antibiotic use in Chilean salmon farming rose from 143.2 t in 2010 to 563.2 t in 2014, then fell to 382.5 t in 2016, when it equalled about 0.53 kg per tonne of harvested salmon [11](https://doi.org/10.3389/fmicb.2018.01284). In 2025, total use was 415.4 t of active ingredient for 1,167,342 t of harvested salmonids. This gives an antimicrobial consumption index (ICA) of 0.036%, or about 360 g per tonne harvested, up from 0.034% in 2024 and down from a peak of 0.063% in 2015. Because both years use harvested biomass, use per tonne harvested fell by about 32% from 2016 to 2025, while harvest grew by about 60% and total use by about 9% [16](https://www.sernapesca.cl/app/uploads/2026/03/Informe-de-uso-de-antimicrobianos-ano-2025.pdf).
    - Most antibiotic use is for SRS, not furunculosis. In sea-phase production cycles closed in 2025, *Piscirickettsia salmonis* accounted for 97.51% of antimicrobial use. The freshwater phase accounted for only 1.17% of national use, but furunculosis was responsible for 7.35% of it [16](https://www.sernapesca.cl/app/uploads/2026/03/Informe-de-uso-de-antimicrobianos-ano-2025.pdf).

- **Treatment & Management:**
    - In 2016, florfenicol (82.5%) and oxytetracycline (16.8%) made up nearly all antibiotic use [11](https://doi.org/10.3389/fmicb.2018.01284). In sea-phase cycles closed in 2025, florfenicol made up 97.57% of use and was the drug of choice for every bacterial disease except tenacibaculosis. It was given almost exclusively in medicated feed. In freshwater, oxytetracycline (60.63%) and florfenicol (39.15%) together made up 99.78% of use. Furunculosis was the third most treated freshwater disease, after flavobacteriosis and [[Bacterial Kidney Disease (Renibacterium salmoninarum)|bacterial kidney disease]] [16](https://www.sernapesca.cl/app/uploads/2026/03/Informe-de-uso-de-antimicrobianos-ano-2025.pdf).
    - PROA-Salmón is SERNAPESCA's voluntary antimicrobial stewardship certification for sea farms. In 2025, it covered 262,416 t, about a quarter of national harvest. Certified cycles had an ICA of 13.47 g per tonne, 96% lower than the average for all sea-phase cycles closed that year (313.81 g/t) [16](https://www.sernapesca.cl/app/uploads/2026/03/Informe-de-uso-de-antimicrobianos-ano-2025.pdf).
    - Data on resistance are limited. A 2018 review found only one study of antibiotic resistance in Chilean *A. salmonicida* [11](https://doi.org/10.3389/fmicb.2018.01284). Since then, a Chilean atypical *A. salmonicida* isolate was found to carry a previously undescribed plasmid with the florfenicol and tetracycline resistance genes *floR* and *tet(A)* [25](https://doi.org/10.3390/microorganisms12010064). One *A. piscicola* isolate was resistant to oxytetracycline (MIC above 64 µg/mL), which was linked to the *tet(E)* gene [24](https://doi.org/10.3390/pathogens15040402).

#### Norway

**Classical furunculosis**

- Classical furunculosis (*A. salmonicida* subsp. *salmonicida*) is a **notifiable disease** in Norway (category F, national diseases), so suspected or confirmed cases must be reported to the Norwegian Food Safety Authority (*Mattilsynet*). After its reintroduction in 1985 it spread to more than 70 rivers. The last detection in wild fish was in 2019 [17](https://www.vetinst.no/sykdom-og-agens/furunkulose).
- In farmed salmonids, the disease was detected at 5 sites in 2021, 2 in 2022 and none in 2023, 2024 or 2025. In lumpfish, it was found at one site in 2022, where it was also detected in salmon, and at three sites in the Namsenfjord area in 2020, where salmon farms in the same area were also affected [18](https://www.vetinst.no/rapporter-og-publikasjoner/rapporter/2026/fiskehelserapporten-2025/_/attachment/inline/363a7595-e0c3-4ee8-87e6-71ba808b4288:b3c8e9bf1ee7902bdc1ab93334e5219666ceabbd/Fiskehelserapporten%202025.pdf) [34](https://www.vetinst.no/rapporter-og-publikasjoner/rapporter/2021/fiskehelserapporten-2020) [36](https://www.vetinst.no/rapporter-og-publikasjoner/rapporter/2023/fiskehelserapporten-2022).
- In the 2025 survey of fish health personnel, no respondent named classical furunculosis as a cause of mortality, poor growth or reduced welfare, or as an increasing problem, in any production phase of salmon or rainbow trout. The situation is under control thanks to biosecurity routines and widespread use of effective vaccines. Occasional outbreaks still occur, and the disease is expected to become more important in a warmer climate, so it should still be monitored and vaccination remains necessary [18](https://www.vetinst.no/rapporter-og-publikasjoner/rapporter/2026/fiskehelserapporten-2025/_/attachment/inline/363a7595-e0c3-4ee8-87e6-71ba808b4288:b3c8e9bf1ee7902bdc1ab93334e5219666ceabbd/Fiskehelserapporten%202025.pdf).

**Atypical furunculosis**

- Atypical furunculosis is **not** notifiable in Norway, so the figures below come from diagnostic work at the Norwegian Veterinary Institute and, in some years, private laboratories. In farmed salmonids, atypical *A. salmonicida* was not detected in 2025. Atypical infections have been uncommon in farmed salmon for many years, because vaccination against subsp. *salmonicida* usually also protects against atypical variants [18](https://www.vetinst.no/rapporter-og-publikasjoner/rapporter/2026/fiskehelserapporten-2025/_/attachment/inline/363a7595-e0c3-4ee8-87e6-71ba808b4288:b3c8e9bf1ee7902bdc1ab93334e5219666ceabbd/Fiskehelserapporten%202025.pdf).

In 2025, emaciation, handling and atypical furunculosis were named as the main causes of mortality, reduced welfare and increasing occurrence in lumpfish kept with salmon [34](https://www.vetinst.no/rapporter-og-publikasjoner/rapporter/2021/fiskehelserapporten-2020) [35](https://www.vetinst.no/rapporter-og-publikasjoner/rapporter/2022/fiskehelserapporten-2021) [36](https://www.vetinst.no/rapporter-og-publikasjoner/rapporter/2023/fiskehelserapporten-2022) [37](https://www.vetinst.no/rapporter-og-publikasjoner/rapporter/2024/fiskehelserapporten-2023) [38](https://www.vetinst.no/rapporter-og-publikasjoner/rapporter/2025/fiskehelserapporten-2024) [18](https://www.vetinst.no/rapporter-og-publikasjoner/rapporter/2026/fiskehelserapporten-2025/_/attachment/inline/363a7595-e0c3-4ee8-87e6-71ba808b4288:b3c8e9bf1ee7902bdc1ab93334e5219666ceabbd/Fiskehelserapporten%202025.pdf).
- Wild-caught wrasse can be healthy carriers. When fish of different origins are mixed in a new and stressful environment, the disease can flare up. No effective vaccine has been developed, and treating sick cleaner fish in sea cages is not practically possible [18](https://www.vetinst.no/rapporter-og-publikasjoner/rapporter/2026/fiskehelserapporten-2025/_/attachment/inline/363a7595-e0c3-4ee8-87e6-71ba808b4288:b3c8e9bf1ee7902bdc1ab93334e5219666ceabbd/Fiskehelserapporten%202025.pdf).
- **Atlantic halibut and other marine species:** Atypical *A. salmonicida* is a common cause of granulomas and bacterial spread in farmed marine species, and it is regularly found in Atlantic halibut (*Hippoglossus hippoglossus*) with disease and increased mortality, often in early life stages. In 2025 it was found, together with *Vibrio anguillarum* O2β and *Vibrio tapetis*, in juvenile halibut with poor appetite and increased mortality. Fish health personnel reported atypical furunculosis as a problem in both halibut and spotted wolffish (*Anarhichas minor*) farms. In halibut, florfenicol treatment has been reported to work well, and a higher water flow combined with a lower water level in tanks helped prevent the disease [34](https://www.vetinst.no/rapporter-og-publikasjoner/rapporter/2021/fiskehelserapporten-2020) [38](https://www.vetinst.no/rapporter-og-publikasjoner/rapporter/2025/fiskehelserapporten-2024) [18](https://www.vetinst.no/rapporter-og-publikasjoner/rapporter/2026/fiskehelserapporten-2025/_/attachment/inline/363a7595-e0c3-4ee8-87e6-71ba808b4288:b3c8e9bf1ee7902bdc1ab93334e5219666ceabbd/Fiskehelserapporten%202025.pdf).
- **Antibiotic use:** Atypical furunculosis is now the main reason for antibiotic treatment of farmed marine fish in Norway. Prescriptions for halibut rose from 12 in 2021 to 24 in 2022, 44 in 2023, 66 in 2024 and 94 in 2025. Atypical furunculosis was the diagnosis on 36 of the 44 halibut prescriptions in 2023, on 66% of marine-species prescriptions in 2024, and on 73% in 2025. In 2025, 98 of the 136 prescriptions for farmed fish (94 for halibut, 4 for cod) went to marine species, accounting for 170 of the 498 kg used. Salmon received 33 prescriptions (322 kg), mainly for yersiniosis and classical vibriosis. Cleaner fish received only four prescriptions in 2025, compared with 126 treatments in 2016 [37](https://www.vetinst.no/rapporter-og-publikasjoner/rapporter/2024/fiskehelserapporten-2023) [38](https://www.vetinst.no/rapporter-og-publikasjoner/rapporter/2025/fiskehelserapporten-2024) [18](https://www.vetinst.no/rapporter-og-publikasjoner/rapporter/2026/fiskehelserapporten-2025/_/attachment/inline/363a7595-e0c3-4ee8-87e6-71ba808b4288:b3c8e9bf1ee7902bdc1ab93334e5219666ceabbd/Fiskehelserapporten%202025.pdf).
- **Atlantic cod:** Cod farming is growing again, with about 19,700 tonnes slaughtered in 2025 compared with 14,400 tonnes in 2024. In cod, atypical furunculosis causes granulomas in internal organs, particularly kidney and spleen, and is an important differential diagnosis for [[Francisellosis (Francisella noatunensis)|francisellosis]]. Atypical *A. salmonicida* was detected at one cod site in 2024, and the 2025 report does not describe any detections in cod. Fish health personnel report a lack of effective vaccines for cod, and autogenous vaccines are used instead [37](https://www.vetinst.no/rapporter-og-publikasjoner/rapporter/2024/fiskehelserapporten-2023) [38](https://www.vetinst.no/rapporter-og-publikasjoner/rapporter/2025/fiskehelserapporten-2024) [18](https://www.vetinst.no/rapporter-og-publikasjoner/rapporter/2026/fiskehelserapporten-2025/_/attachment/inline/363a7595-e0c3-4ee8-87e6-71ba808b4288:b3c8e9bf1ee7902bdc1ab93334e5219666ceabbd/Fiskehelserapporten%202025.pdf).
- **Wild fish:** In 2023, atypical *A. salmonicida* was found in a wild Atlantic salmon in the river Tana. The fish had pale gills and organs and pinpoint haemorrhages in the liver [37](https://www.vetinst.no/rapporter-og-publikasjoner/rapporter/2024/fiskehelserapporten-2023).

#### Scotland

- **Furunculosis Incidence and Importance:**
    - Furunculosis is **not** a listed (notifiable) disease in Scotland. The Scottish Government's list of listed fish diseases and their status (BKD, EHN, *Gyrodactylus salaris*, IHN, ISA, KHV, SVC and VHS) does not include it [41](https://www.gov.scot/publications/health-status-of-fish-and-shellfish-diseases-in-scotland/).
    - Classical furunculosis had a devastating impact on Scottish salmonid farming until the late 1980s. Latently infected Scottish smolts also reintroduced it to Norway in 1985 [17](https://www.vetinst.no/sykdom-og-agens/furunkulose). The Scottish Government credits vaccines, including one developed at the Marine Laboratory in Aberdeen, with reducing catastrophic outbreaks to small isolated incidents. Vaccines are still widely used, often together with antimicrobials [40](https://www.gov.scot/publications/diseases-of-wild-and-farmed-finfish/pages/aeromonas-salmonicida/). However, Scottish data from 1984 to 1995 show that the decline in outbreaks began before oil-adjuvanted vaccines were introduced, so other factors probably contributed [42](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0044-8486(00)00384-7).
    - Outbreaks still occur occasionally. In 2023, the FHI recorded *A. salmonicida* as the primary pathogen at a marine site after freshwater treatments, in salmon vaccinated against it. Most deaths were acute with few external signs, while chronically infected fish had furuncles [45](https://storage.googleapis.com/inspection_case_information/2023-0080_FS0361.pdf). In 2025, furunculosis was detected at a recirculating smolt unit by water eDNA, PCR and bacteriology, and the fish were treated with in-feed florfenicol [46](https://storage.googleapis.com/inspection_case_information/2025-0337_FS1361.pdf).
    - Atypical furunculosis is mainly a cleaner fish problem. In farmed and wild ballan wrasse sampled from Scottish hatcheries and salmon farms in 2016 to 2018, atypical *A. salmonicida* *vapA* type V was the most common bacterium (36.8% of positive fish). Farm-specific (autogenous) multivalent vaccines are widely used in wrasse, and an injectable autogenous vaccine gave 79 to 95% relative survival against two *vapA* type V isolates in challenge trials [43](https://doi.org/10.3354/dao03489) [44](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.fsi.2020.09.040).

#### Canada

- **Furunculosis Incidence and Importance:**
    - Furunculosis is an **annually notifiable** disease under the Canadian Food Inspection Agency (CFIA). It is present in Canada and of concern to trading partners, and only laboratories must notify the CFIA when they suspect or diagnose it [47](https://inspection.canada.ca/en/animal-health/aquatic-animals/diseases/annually-notifiable).
    - In Quebec, classical furunculosis is the most common infection in fish farms, especially brook trout farms, and causes 30 to 60% of reported outbreaks each year. Among 100 Canadian *A. salmonicida* subsp. *salmonicida* isolates, 37% carried at least one antibiotic resistance plasmid, and the multiresistance plasmid pSN254b (25% of isolates) has been found in Quebec, New Brunswick and Nova Scotia [7](https://doi.org/10.1038/srep35617).
    - In Atlantic Canada (New Brunswick, Nova Scotia, Newfoundland and Labrador, Prince Edward Island), atypical *A. salmonicida* was found in 1.66% of 2,291 salmonid diagnostic cases submitted to a regional laboratory from 2000 to 2021. It was detected mainly in the last decade, at low frequency in Atlantic salmon and not at all in rainbow trout, and more than 90% of tested isolates were resistant to oxytetracycline. Detection of the main bacterial pathogens declined over the period [49](https://doi.org/10.3390/vetsci9040159).
    - In British Columbia, *A. salmonicida* is endemic in wild and farmed salmon. On Atlantic salmon farms in the Discovery Islands area, it was reported in six of the years from 2002 to 2017, on seven farms. Vaccination is voluntary, but the main companies vaccinate all fish. Even so, furunculosis can develop after handling or transfer stress if carriers were present before vaccination. A DFO risk assessment concluded that the bacterium on these farms poses minimal risk to Fraser River sockeye salmon [48](https://waves-vagues.dfo-mpo.gc.ca/library-bibliotheque/40947701.pdf).

## Conclusion

Furunculosis remains one of the most consequential bacterial diseases in global salmonid aquaculture. Norway's vaccination programme of the early 1990s, together with biosecurity measures, brought classical furunculosis under control and reduced antibacterial use in fish farming from about 48 tonnes to about 1 tonne per year [17](https://www.vetinst.no/sykdom-og-agens/furunkulose) [32](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-2109.2010.02726.x). Yet the disease persists because of *A. salmonicida*'s ability to establish subclinical carrier states, its environmental persistence, incomplete protection from current vaccines against emerging antigenic variants, and the spread of plasmid-borne antimicrobial resistance genes [2](https://doi.org/10.3389/fcimb.2025.1508135) [7](https://doi.org/10.1038/srep35617) [8](https://doi.org/10.7717/peerj.2595) [9](https://doi.org/10.3390/microorganisms9061201). Management today rests on vaccination, biosecurity and minimising stress during handling, transport and sorting [17](https://www.vetinst.no/sykdom-og-agens/furunkulose) [32](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-2109.2010.02726.x).
