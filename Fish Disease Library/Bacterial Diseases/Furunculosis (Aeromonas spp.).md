## Overview

### What is Furunculosis (Aeromonas salmonicida)?

Furunculosis is a bacterial disease of salmonid fish caused by *Aeromonas salmonicida*, a Gram-negative, non-motile rod first isolated in 1894 from brown trout in Germany, making it one of the earliest described bacterial fish diseases [1](https://doi.org/10.1111/1751-7915.12091). The disease takes its name from its classic chronic presentation: raised, fluid-filled boil-like lesions (furuncles) in the musculature that ulcerate and rupture. It primarily affects Atlantic salmon (*Salmo salar*), rainbow trout (*Oncorhynchus mykiss*), and other salmonids, and remains one of the most economically important bacterial diseases in salmon farming, with major impacts in Norway, Scotland, Chile, Canada, and the Faroe Islands [1](https://doi.org/10.1111/1751-7915.12091) [2](https://doi.org/10.3389/fcimb.2025.1508135).

*A. salmonicida* is virtually ubiquitous in farmed salmonid populations, often persisting as a subclinical carrier infection that can flare into clinical disease under stress such as seawater transfer (smoltification) [1](https://doi.org/10.1111/1751-7915.12091). Alongside "typical" *A. salmonicida* subsp. *salmonicida*, a range of "atypical" strains and closely related species, including a re-emerging complex implicated in Chile's salmon industry, can produce a similar disease picture and complicate diagnosis and vaccination in some regions [17](https://doi.org/10.3390/microorganisms10010189) [18](https://doi.org/10.1093/femsle/fnz074) [19](https://doi.org/10.3390/microorganisms12010064) [20](https://doi.org/10.3390/pathogens15040402). The introduction of oil-adjuvanted vaccines in the early 1990s transformed disease management in Norway, but furunculosis remains a persistent challenge where vaccine coverage is incomplete or emerging strains partially escape vaccine-induced protection [1](https://doi.org/10.1111/1751-7915.12091) [2](https://doi.org/10.3389/fcimb.2025.1508135).

## Clinical Signs of Furunculosis (Aeromonas salmonicida)

### Common Signs

- **Physical Signs:**
    - **Furuncles (Muscular Boils):** Characteristic raised, hemorrhagic swellings in the musculature that rupture to form deep, necrotic ulcers, most prominent in the chronic form of the disease; may be absent in peracute or acute presentations [1](https://doi.org/10.1111/1751-7915.12091) [5](https://doi.org/10.3390/biology14101330).
    - **Hemorrhages at Fin Bases and Operculum:** Petechial and ecchymotic hemorrhages, particularly at the base of pectoral fins, opercular margins, and the vent, are consistent signs of systemic bacteraemia [1](https://doi.org/10.1111/1751-7915.12091) [5](https://doi.org/10.3390/biology14101330).
    - **Exophthalmia:** Unilateral or bilateral protrusion of the eyes due to retrobulbar fluid accumulation is commonly observed [5](https://doi.org/10.3390/biology14101330).
    - **Bloody Discharge from Vent:** Hemorrhagic enteritis can produce bloody mucoid discharge from the anus, a sign associated with acute and subacute disease [1](https://doi.org/10.1111/1751-7915.12091).
    - **Splenomegaly and Hepatic Necrosis:** Post-mortem examination typically reveals a markedly enlarged, congested spleen and a friable liver with focal areas of necrosis [5](https://doi.org/10.3390/biology14101330).
    - **Renal Oedema and Congestion:** The kidneys show oedema with petechial haemorrhages and congestion; renal softening and discoloration are common post-mortem findings in conjunction with hepatic and splenic pathology [5](https://doi.org/10.3390/biology14101330).

- **Behavioral Changes:**
    - **Lethargy:** Reduced swimming activity; affected fish isolate from the shoal and swim near the surface or cage edges [1](https://doi.org/10.1111/1751-7915.12091).
    - **Loss of Appetite:** Rapid and marked decline in feed intake is often the first sign noticed by farm staff [1](https://doi.org/10.1111/1751-7915.12091).
    - **Darkening of Skin:** Affected fish frequently display abnormal melanization (darkening) of body coloration [1](https://doi.org/10.1111/1751-7915.12091).

**Progression of Clinical Signs**

- **Early Stages (Peracute/Acute):** Fish may die suddenly with minimal external signs. Peracute disease presents as rapid systemic bacteraemia with hemorrhages but no furuncle formation. In acute disease, fish show lethargy, loss of appetite, and fin-base hemorrhages. Mortality rises steeply over a 1–3 day period [1](https://doi.org/10.1111/1751-7915.12091) [5](https://doi.org/10.3390/biology14101330).
- **Advanced Stages (Subacute/Chronic):** In subacute and chronic infections, characteristic furuncles develop in the musculature, progressing to open ulcers. The clinical course is more protracted, with fish surviving longer but serving as ongoing sources of pathogen shedding. Stress events, such as seawater transfer (smoltification), handling, crowding, temperature change, or concurrent parasitic infestations, commonly trigger transition from latent carrier to clinically diseased states [1](https://doi.org/10.1111/1751-7915.12091).
- **Impact on Fish Health:** Beyond direct mortality, furunculosis causes significant production losses through reduced growth, increased feed conversion ratio, carcass downgrades due to furuncle damage, and costs associated with treatment and mortality removal. Fish that survive clinical episodes may remain chronic carriers that re-shed the pathogen under subsequent stress events [1](https://doi.org/10.1111/1751-7915.12091).
- **Atypical and Related-Agent Presentations:** Re-emergent atypical *A. salmonicida* outbreaks in Chilean Atlantic salmon have presented with periorbital hemorrhages, fin-base and abdominal hemorrhages, furuncles, skin ulcers, hepatic/splenic/renal petechiae, and hemorrhagic enteritis, a picture largely indistinguishable from typical disease on gross examination [19](https://doi.org/10.3390/microorganisms12010064). *Aeromonas piscicola*, a distinct species causing furunculosis-like disease in Chilean *S. salar*, produces a comparable disease picture under experimental challenge: focal cutaneous ulcerative lesions, marked visceral congestion, and septicemia with mortality exceeding 80% by 4 days post-infection for the more virulent isolates [20](https://doi.org/10.3390/pathogens15040402).

### Causes of Furunculosis (Aeromonas salmonicida)

**Etiology**

- **Causative Agent:** *Aeromonas salmonicida* subsp. *salmonicida* is a Gram-negative, non-motile, non-spore-forming, facultatively anaerobic rod (1.0–1.5 × 0.8–1.0 μm). A hallmark of typical strains is the production of a brown, water-soluble diffusible pigment (a hydroxamate-type melanin-like compound) on tryptic soy agar (TSA), which serves as a useful phenotypic identifier. The organism grows optimally at 22–25°C and poorly above 32°C, explaining the typical occurrence of outbreaks in cooler water conditions. Isolates possess an ordered crystalline surface protein layer (A-layer or S-layer), composed of a 49 kDa protein subunit encoded by the *vapA* gene, which is a critical virulence determinant and a key target for diagnostic and vaccine development [1](https://doi.org/10.1111/1751-7915.12091) [3](https://doi.org/10.3390/md13063791). All typical and atypical *A. salmonicida* strains with the exception of subsp. *pectinolytica* share the same LPS O-antigen biosynthesis cluster and presence of the A-layer; subsp. *pectinolytica* strains, which lack both the O-antigen cluster and A-layer, are the only subspecies not reported as fish pathogens [3](https://doi.org/10.3390/md13063791).
- **Typical vs. Atypical Strains:** Classical or "typical" furunculosis is caused by *A. salmonicida* subsp. *salmonicida* and primarily affects salmonid species including Atlantic salmon (*Salmo salar*), rainbow trout (*Oncorhynchus mykiss*), brown trout (*Salmo trutta*), brook trout (*Salvelinus fontinalis*), and Arctic char (*Salvelinus alpinus*). "Atypical" *A. salmonicida*, historically defined by the subspecies *achromogenes*, *masoucida*, *smithia*, and *pectinolytica*, affects a broader range of non-salmonid species and is typically slower-growing, non-pigmented, and less acutely virulent than typical strains [1](https://doi.org/10.1111/1751-7915.12091). However, comparative whole-genome analyses show that this subspecies classification does not map cleanly onto genomic divergence: strains with an atypical phenotype can share ~99.5% genome identity with typical subsp. *salmonicida* isolates, differing mainly through insertion-sequence activity and a reduced complement of plasmid-borne virulence genes that produces a more chronic disease course [17](https://doi.org/10.3390/microorganisms10010189). A global *vapA* A-layer genotyping survey of 675 isolates from 50 fish species across 26 countries similarly found that atypical genotypes are not confined to a handful of host species but show broad, host and geography-linked distribution, in some cases spread between countries via live fish movements [18](https://doi.org/10.1093/femsle/fnz074).
- **Atypical Strains and the *A. piscicola* Complex:** Atypical isolates often deviate from the classical phenotype in growth temperature and motility. An atypical *A. salmonicida* strain isolated from a Siberian sturgeon (*Acipenser baerii*) skin ulcer showed unusual motility and growth at ≥37°C while still producing typical hemorrhagic septicaemia and ulceration, illustrating that atypical strains can occupy warmer thermal niches than the classical psychrophilic profile [21](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.heliyon.2023.e17775). In Chile, a related but taxonomically distinct species, *Aeromonas piscicola*, has been isolated from freshwater furunculosis outbreaks in *S. salar* and rainbow trout since 2022; these *A. salmonicida*-like isolates fail to amplify the *vapA* and *fstA* qPCR markers used for routine species confirmation, and whole-genome and phylogenetic analysis places them within *A. piscicola* rather than *A. salmonicida* despite sharing common surface antigens [20](https://doi.org/10.3390/pathogens15040402).
- **Key Virulence Factors:** The pathogenicity of *A. salmonicida* is multifactorial. Key virulence determinants include: the A-layer surface protein (VapA), which confers resistance to phagocytosis and serum killing; a Type III Secretion System (T3SS) that injects effector proteins directly into host cells to induce a transient immunosuppressive state, facilitating systemic spread [1](https://doi.org/10.1111/1751-7915.12091); siderophores (amonabactin and acinetobactin) enabling iron acquisition in host tissues; a Type II Secretion System (T2SS) that exports extracellular enzymes including serine protease AsaP1, lipase, and glycerophospholipid-cholesterol acyltransferase (GCAT) — the latter responsible for erythrocyte lysis and tissue destruction [1](https://doi.org/10.1111/1751-7915.12091). Cold shock proteins CspB and CspD also contribute to virulence by acting as transcriptional regulators of key virulence loci in the chromosome and the large pASal virulence plasmid [4](https://doi.org/10.1128/iai.00011-24).
- **Transmission Methods:**
    - **Horizontal Transmission:** The primary route. *A. salmonicida* is shed continuously by infected and carrier fish into the surrounding water via feces, mucus, and from ulcer sites. Infection occurs via the gills, skin abrasions, or the oral route. Waterborne transmission within a cage or net pen is extremely efficient at typical aquaculture stocking densities. Genomic epidemiological analysis of outbreak isolates from multiple aquaculture facilities has confirmed that spatially dispersed farms can share a single epidemiological unit, consistent with waterborne inter-site spread [10](https://doi.org/10.3390/pathogens13100908).
    - **Vertical Transmission:** Not definitively established for *A. salmonicida* subsp. *salmonicida*. Contamination of egg surfaces at spawning from carrier parents is possible, but true transovarial vertical transmission has not been demonstrated [1](https://doi.org/10.1111/1751-7915.12091).
    - **Vector/Environmental Transmission:** The pathogen can persist in freshwater sediment and river biofilm for weeks. Wild salmonids and sea-run fish may introduce the pathogen to previously unaffected farms. Ectoparasites (e.g., *Lepeophtheirus salmonis*, sea lice) may compromise skin integrity and facilitate bacterial entry, though they are not established as direct vectors [1](https://doi.org/10.1111/1751-7915.12091).

**Risk Factors**

- **Environmental Factors:** Water temperature between 12°C and 20°C is most favorable for disease expression; outbreaks in sea-cage Atlantic salmon commonly occur in spring and early autumn when sea temperatures are in this range. Rapid temperature changes and acute warming events can precipitate outbreaks in carrier populations. Poor water quality and reduced dissolved oxygen further compromise immune competence [1](https://doi.org/10.1111/1751-7915.12091). Cold shock protein-mediated regulation of virulence genes indicates that the psychrotrophic biology of *A. salmonicida* is intrinsically linked to its pathogenicity at cold and transitional water temperatures [4](https://doi.org/10.1128/iai.00011-24).
- **Farm Management Practices:** Seawater transfer (smoltification stress) is a major trigger for clinical outbreaks in previously subclinical carrier fish. High stocking density, handling, crowding, sorting, and the concurrent presence of sea lice infestations or other parasites all elevate susceptibility. Incomplete vaccination coverage or inadequate vaccine storage and administration practices reduce immune protection [1](https://doi.org/10.1111/1751-7915.12091).

### Diagnosis

**Diagnostic Methods**

- **Clinical Examination:** A presumptive diagnosis of furunculosis can be made from characteristic gross pathology in salmonids, particularly the combination of furuncles, fin-base hemorrhages, exophthalmia, bloody vent discharge, and splenomegaly. However, peracute and acute presentations may lack furuncles entirely, and clinical signs overlap significantly with those of other bacterial septicaemias. Isolation of the causative agent is required for definitive diagnosis [1](https://doi.org/10.1111/1751-7915.12091).
- **Laboratory Tests:**
    - **Bacterial Culture:** *A. salmonicida* grows readily on standard bacteriological media (TSA, blood agar) at 22°C. Incubation at temperatures above 28°C should be avoided as it may inhibit growth. Typical subsp. *salmonicida* colonies produce a characteristic diffuse brown pigment visible after 48–72 hours of incubation — the most practical rapid phenotypic identifier in field-oriented laboratories. Oxidase-positive, glucose-fermentative, and negative for motility [1](https://doi.org/10.1111/1751-7915.12091).
    - **PCR-Based Methods:** PCR targeting the *vapA* gene (encoding the A-layer VapA protein) is the standard molecular confirmation tool for typical *A. salmonicida*. Quantitative PCR (qPCR) assays allow load estimation and are useful for surveillance of subclinical carrier fish without clinical signs. Multiplex PCR assays can simultaneously detect and differentiate *A. salmonicida* subsp. *salmonicida* from *Flavobacterium psychrophilum* and *Yersinia ruckeri* in a single reaction, with detection limits of 30 CFU for *A. salmonicida* in pure culture and 250 CFU in the presence of tissue debris [6](https://doi.org/10.4142/jvs.2010.11.3.235). Importantly, *vapA*-based PCR targets may fail to amplify in emerging *vapA*-absent strains now circulating in Chilean salmon farms, underscoring the need to complement standard assays with culture or broader-spectrum molecular screening [2](https://doi.org/10.3389/fcimb.2025.1508135). The same limitation affects *A. piscicola*: isolates causing furunculosis-like disease in Chilean salmon farms also fail to amplify both *vapA* and *fstA* qPCR markers, so colonies with an *A. salmonicida*-like appearance that test negative on these assays require whole-genome sequencing or phylogenetic analysis for definitive species identification rather than being dismissed as non-pathogenic [20](https://doi.org/10.3390/pathogens15040402) [19](https://doi.org/10.3390/microorganisms12010064). For broader subtyping of atypical and typical isolates alike, *vapA* A-layer genotyping provides a standardized epidemiological marker that has been applied across hundreds of isolates internationally [18](https://doi.org/10.1093/femsle/fnz074).
    - **Serological Methods:** Indirect fluorescent antibody test (IFAT) and ELISA-based assays using *A. salmonicida*-specific antisera have been used for pathogen detection and for monitoring vaccine-induced antibody responses. Serum agglutination titers can serve as a proxy measure of immune protection post-vaccination [1](https://doi.org/10.1111/1751-7915.12091).
    - **Histopathology:** Histological sections from muscle, liver, spleen, and kidney reveal necrosis and bacterial aggregates. Muscle tissue around furuncles shows extensive myonecrosis with few inflammatory cells, reflecting the immunoevasive properties of the A-layer. Hepatic and renal necrosis with bacteria in sinusoids and tubular lumina are consistent acute systemic findings, with significant organ dysfunction and persistent pathological changes documented even after the resolution of peak bacteraemia [5](https://doi.org/10.3390/biology14101330).

**Differential Diagnosis**

- **Distinguishing Furunculosis from Other Diseases:** Clinical signs of furunculosis overlap substantially with those of vibriosis (*Vibrio* spp.), yersiniosis (*Yersinia ruckeri*), bacterial kidney disease (BKD, *Renibacterium salmoninarum*), winter ulcer disease (*Moritella viscosa*), and haemorrhagic septicaemia caused by *Aeromonas hydrophila* (motile aeromonads). The non-motility of *A. salmonicida* subsp. *salmonicida* and the characteristic brown pigment on agar distinguish it from motile aeromonads. Multiplex PCR simultaneously discriminates *A. salmonicida* from *F. psychrophilum* and *Y. ruckeri*, enabling unambiguous exclusion of the most common co-circulating bacterial pathogens in a single reaction [6](https://doi.org/10.4142/jvs.2010.11.3.235).

### Treatment and Prevention

**Treatment Options**

- **Current Treatments:**
    - **Antibiotics:** Florfenicol (administered orally via medicated feed) is the primary licensed treatment in most major salmon-producing countries. Oxytetracycline is also approved in several jurisdictions. Historically, sulfonamide-trimethoprim combinations were widely used. Antibiotic treatment does not eliminate the carrier state and is used primarily to reduce acute mortality and bacterial shedding during an active outbreak [1](https://doi.org/10.1111/1751-7915.12091).
    - **Treatment Challenges:** Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) in *A. salmonicida* is a growing concern. Resistance to tetracyclines (mediated by *tetA* and *tetB* genes on large resistance plasmids) and sulfonamides (*sul1*) is encoded predominantly on conjugative multiresistance plasmids, notably pSN254b — the dominant AMR plasmid in Canadian isolates — and variants of the pAsa4 plasmid carrying chloramphenicol, sulfonamide, and tetracycline resistance genes whose mosaic architecture is shaped by mobile genetic elements [7](https://doi.org/10.1038/srep35617) [8](https://doi.org/10.7717/peerj.2595). Antibiotic use in fish farms creates selection pressure that increases the occurrence and abundance of multiresistant *Aeromonas* strains in both fish and their surrounding water environments [9](https://doi.org/10.3390/microorganisms9061201). A critical challenge is that antibiotic therapy does not eliminate latent carrier fish, which remain a reservoir for re-introduction of the pathogen into clean populations after treatment ends [1](https://doi.org/10.1111/1751-7915.12091). Atypical and related agents add further AMR complexity in Chile: re-emergent atypical psychrophilic *A. salmonicida* isolates carry a novel, previously undescribed plasmid alongside known virulence and resistance factors [19](https://doi.org/10.3390/microorganisms12010064), and at least one *A. piscicola* isolate from a Chilean furunculosis-like outbreak showed a markedly elevated oxytetracycline MIC (>64 µg/mL) relative to co-circulating strains, indicating high-level resistance to a first-line treatment [20](https://doi.org/10.3390/pathogens15040402).
- **Vaccines:**
    - **Commercial Vaccines:** Oil-adjuvanted injectable furunculosis vaccines (administered intraperitoneally to juvenile salmon prior to seawater transfer) have been commercially available since the early 1990s. These vaccines, combined with multivalent formulations covering other key pathogens (e.g., ISA virus, *Vibrio* spp., *M. viscosa*), are the cornerstone of disease prevention in Norwegian, Scottish, and Chilean salmon farming. Deployment of furunculosis vaccines in Norway in the early 1990s resulted in a reduction in antibiotic use of approximately 99% and is the defining example of how vaccination can transform antibiotic stewardship in aquaculture [1](https://doi.org/10.1111/1751-7915.12091).
    - **Vaccine Limitations:** Current oil-adjuvanted vaccines are formulated on the basis of standard *A. salmonicida* antigenic profiles, including the VapA protein as a key immunogen. A newly emerged *vapA*-absent strain in Chilean salmon farms elicits a reduced serological response in vaccinated fish, suggesting that a growing proportion of recent furunculosis cases may be caused by a strain that partially escapes existing vaccine-induced immunity [2](https://doi.org/10.3389/fcimb.2025.1508135). A parallel cross-protection gap exists for *A. piscicola*: vaccinated salmon mount a strong IgM response against the homologous *A. salmonicida vapA*+ antigen in the commercial vaccine, but only a modest IgM response against *A. piscicola* despite shared surface antigens, indicating limited heterologous protection against this related agent [20](https://doi.org/10.3390/pathogens15040402). Additionally, while oil-adjuvanted bacterin vaccines provide significant protection, they are associated with intraperitoneal granulomatous reactions and spinal deformities. Alternative adjuvant formulations — including CpG oligodeoxynucleotides and the liposomal CAF01 system — reduce the severity and frequency of these adverse effects while maintaining protective efficacy comparable to the mineral oil benchmark [13](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-017-06324-7). Autogenous vaccine strategies, supported by whole-genome sequencing evidence that outbreak isolates from geographically dispersed farms often form a single epidemiological unit, offer a complementary prophylactic tool where commercial vaccine coverage is imperfect [10](https://doi.org/10.3390/pathogens13100908).

**Preventive Measures**

- **Biosecurity Protocols:**
    - All juvenile salmon should receive a full vaccination course with an oil-adjuvanted intraperitoneal injection of a licensed furunculosis vaccine prior to seawater transfer, with correct administration technique to ensure efficacy [1](https://doi.org/10.1111/1751-7915.12091).
    - Certified health screening of broodstock and incoming smolts using culture and PCR to detect covert carriers before transfer into sea cages — noting that *vapA* PCR screening should be supplemented with culture in regions where the *vapA*-absent strain has been detected [2](https://doi.org/10.3389/fcimb.2025.1508135).
    - Fallowing and disinfection of net pen sites between production cycles to reduce environmental bacterial load [1](https://doi.org/10.1111/1751-7915.12091).
    - Genomic epidemiological characterization of outbreak isolates supports the application of site-specific autogenous vaccines to aquaculture facilities sharing a confirmed epidemiological link [10](https://doi.org/10.3390/pathogens13100908).

- **Farm Management Practices:**
    - Minimizing handling, crowding, and other stressors during the peri-smoltification period, when carrier fish are most likely to transition to clinical disease [1](https://doi.org/10.1111/1751-7915.12091).
    - Integrated parasite management (particularly sea lice control) to maintain skin integrity and reduce entry points for *A. salmonicida* [1](https://doi.org/10.1111/1751-7915.12091).
    - Surveillance of wild salmonid populations in proximity to farms where they may act as a reservoir and re-introduction source [1](https://doi.org/10.1111/1751-7915.12091).

### Case Studies

**Real-World Examples**

- **Notable Outbreaks:**
    - **Norway (1980s–Early 1990s) — The Pre-Vaccine Epidemic:** The Norwegian salmon farming industry experienced devastating furunculosis losses during this period as sea-cage production expanded rapidly. The near-universal deployment of oil-adjuvanted intraperitoneal furunculosis vaccines in the early 1990s achieved a >99% reduction in antibiotic use within the sector — arguably the most dramatic demonstration of vaccine-mediated antibiotic stewardship in the history of aquaculture [1](https://doi.org/10.1111/1751-7915.12091). The Norwegian experience remains the foundational case study for the role of vaccination in managing this disease at a national scale.
    - **Chile (Ongoing) — Furunculosis in the World's Second-Largest Salmon Producer:** Chile's salmon farming sector experienced a steep rise in antibiotic consumption through the mid-2010s, reaching 382.5 tonnes in 2016 (~0.53 kg per tonne of harvested salmon) [11](https://doi.org/10.3389/fmicb.2018.01284). Sustained regulatory pressure, improved disease management, and expanded vaccination coverage have since driven a substantial reduction in treatment intensity: by 2025, total antimicrobial use reached 415.4 tonnes on a production base of 1.167 million tonnes of biomass, corresponding to an Antimicrobial Consumption Index (ICA) of 0.036% (360 g/tonne produced) — a 93% reduction in intensity relative to the 2016 peak despite a 60% increase in production volume [15](https://www.sernapesca.cl) [16](https://www.sernapesca.cl). *Piscirickettsia salmonis* (SRS) remains the dominant driver of antibiotic use, accounting for 97.51% of antimicrobial consumption in closed sea-phase production cycles in 2025, with furunculosis contributing a further 0.25% of sea-phase use and 7.35% of freshwater antibiotic use [16](https://www.sernapesca.cl). SERNAPESCA's passive surveillance detected *A. salmonicida* (typical form) in 284 positive diagnostic reports from freshwater facilities and 238 from marine farms in 2025, ranking it the fourth most frequently identified pathogen in Chilean freshwater salmon production [15](https://www.sernapesca.cl). Both typical and atypical (*vapA*-absent) strains are circulating: atypical *A. salmonicida* generated a further 33 freshwater and 31 marine positive reports, with confirmed detections across Los Lagos, Aysén, and Magallanes [15](https://www.sernapesca.cl). The *vapA*-absent strain characterized in 2025 shows profoundly altered outer membrane protein and LPS antigenic profiles, causing failure of standard *vapA* PCR diagnostics and a reduced serological response to current commercial vaccines — raising concern that a growing proportion of Chilean furunculosis outbreaks may be partially vaccine-escape events [2](https://doi.org/10.3389/fcimb.2025.1508135). Recent work indicates the rise in Chilean furunculosis cannot be ascribed solely to this less-virulent *vapA*-absent strain: whole-genome sequencing of re-emergent isolates (ASA04, ASA05, CIBA_5017) has identified atypical psychrophilic lineages carrying a novel, previously undescribed plasmid alongside known virulence and resistance factors [19](https://doi.org/10.3390/microorganisms12010064), and a proportion of *A. salmonicida*-like, *vapA*/*fstA*-negative colonies from freshwater outbreaks since 2022 have been reclassified as *Aeromonas piscicola* — a distinct species reproducing the full furunculosis phenotype with comparable virulence and only modest vaccine cross-protection [20](https://doi.org/10.3390/pathogens15040402).

- **Lessons Learned:** The Norwegian experience demonstrates that investment in vaccination programs can achieve near-elimination of antibiotic reliance for a major bacterial pathogen. The emergence of the *vapA*-absent strain in Chile illustrates that even highly effective vaccines can face challenges as pathogen populations evolve antigenic variation — underscoring the ongoing need for molecular surveillance of circulating strains, adaptation of diagnostic targets, and updating of vaccine antigens [2](https://doi.org/10.3389/fcimb.2025.1508135). The additional identification of re-emergent atypical *A. salmonicida* lineages and *A. piscicola* as contributors to the same Chilean furunculosis resurgence reinforces that "furunculosis" in this context is better understood as a complex of related agents rather than a single strain, with each agent requiring its own diagnostic targets and vaccine antigens [19](https://doi.org/10.3390/microorganisms12010064) [20](https://doi.org/10.3390/pathogens15040402). Genomic analysis of outbreak isolates supports linking geographically separated facilities into shared epidemiological units, enabling autogenous vaccine strategies for farm clusters that face recurring furunculosis where commercial vaccines provide incomplete protection [10](https://doi.org/10.3390/pathogens13100908).

## Data Insights

### Disease Impact by Country

#### Chile

- **Furunculosis Incidence:**
    - *A. salmonicida* is recognized as a persistent challenge in both freshwater smolt production and sea-phase grow-out in Chilean Atlantic salmon farms. Under SERNAPESCA's passive surveillance program, typical *A. salmonicida* generated **284 positive diagnostic reports from freshwater facilities** and **238 from marine farms** in 2025 — ranking it the **fourth most frequently detected pathogen** in Chilean freshwater salmon production (after PRV, *Flavobacterium psychrophilum*, and IPNV) [15](https://www.sernapesca.cl). Detection is distributed across all major production regions, with the highest diagnostic burden in Los Lagos (154 freshwater reports), Magallanes (76 freshwater; 53 marine reports), and Aysén (42 freshwater; 103 marine reports) [15](https://www.sernapesca.cl).
    - Atypical *A. salmonicida* strains (reported as "*A. salmonicida* atípico") generated a further 33 positive freshwater and 31 marine reports in 2025, with confirmed detections in Los Lagos, Aysén, and Magallanes [15](https://www.sernapesca.cl). An emerging *vapA*-absent strain, first characterized in 2025, is responsible for a rising proportion of furunculosis diagnoses and causes diagnostic failure with standard *vapA* PCR-based screening protocols; its profoundly altered outer membrane protein and LPS profiles suggest it arose through a complex genomic rearrangement (indel) encompassing the entire *vapA* locus [2](https://doi.org/10.3389/fcimb.2025.1508135).
    - The atypical/non-classical furunculosis complex extends beyond this single *vapA*-absent strain. Whole-genome sequencing of additional Chilean isolates has characterized re-emergent atypical psychrophilic *A. salmonicida* lineages carrying a novel virulence/resistance plasmid not previously described in the species [19](https://doi.org/10.3390/microorganisms12010064). Separately, a portion of *A. salmonicida*-like, *vapA*/*fstA*-negative colonies recovered from freshwater furunculosis outbreaks since 2022 has been reclassified — via whole-genome and phylogenetic analysis — as *Aeromonas piscicola*, a distinct species that, in challenge trials, produced focal cutaneous ulcerative lesions, visceral congestion, and septicemia with mortality exceeding 80% by 4 days post-infection for the more virulent isolates, with one isolate showing a markedly elevated oxytetracycline MIC (>64 µg/mL) [20](https://doi.org/10.3390/pathogens15040402). Together, these findings suggest the true diagnostic burden of "atypical furunculosis" in Chile is likely underrepresented by counts based on *vapA*-dependent assays alone.

- **Economic Impact:**
    - Chile consumed 382.5 tonnes of antibiotics in salmon farming in 2016 (~0.53 kg per tonne of harvested salmon), up from 143.2 t in 2010 [11](https://doi.org/10.3389/fmicb.2018.01284). By 2025, total consumption stood at 415.4 tonnes, but against a 60% larger production base (1.167 million tonnes biomass), the ICA had fallen to 0.036% (360 g/tonne produced) — a 93% reduction in treatment intensity from the 2016 peak [16](https://www.sernapesca.cl). While *Piscirickettsia salmonis* drives ~97.5% of sea-cycle antibiotic use, furunculosis accounted for 7.35% of freshwater antibiotic use and 0.25% of sea-phase consumption in 2025, confirming its persistent but secondary role in the national antimicrobial burden [16](https://www.sernapesca.cl).

- **Treatment & Management:**
    - Florfenicol (82.5% of total antibiotic use in 2016) and oxytetracycline (16.8%) have historically dominated Chilean salmon farming therapeutics [11](https://doi.org/10.3389/fmicb.2018.01284). By 2025, florfenicol accounted for 97.57% of antimicrobial use in closed sea cycles, administered almost exclusively via medicated feed; furunculosis treatment follows this pattern almost entirely [16](https://www.sernapesca.cl). In the freshwater phase, oxytetracycline (60.63%) and florfenicol (39.15%) together represent 99.78% of antibiotic use, with furunculosis the third most treated disease after flavobacteriosis and renibacteriosis [16](https://www.sernapesca.cl). SERNAPESCA's voluntary PROA-Salmón antimicrobial stewardship certification covered 262,416 tonnes (approximately 25% of 2025 national output), achieving an ICA of 13.47 g/tonne — 96% below the national sea-phase average — demonstrating that best-practice management can dramatically reduce treatment intensity [16](https://www.sernapesca.cl). The limited scientific research on resistance gene epidemiology in Chilean *A. salmonicida* isolates and the emergence of a *vapA*-absent vaccine-escape strain represent significant knowledge gaps requiring urgent investigation [2](https://doi.org/10.3389/fcimb.2025.1508135) [11](https://doi.org/10.3389/fmicb.2018.01284).

## Conclusion

Furunculosis remains one of the most consequential bacterial diseases in global salmonid aquaculture. The Norwegian vaccination success story of the early 1990s, which cut antibiotic use by roughly 99% remains a landmark example of how vaccination can transform disease management [1](https://doi.org/10.1111/1751-7915.12091). Yet the disease persists because of *A. salmonicida*'s ability to establish subclinical carrier states, its environmental persistence, incomplete protection from current vaccines against emerging antigenic variants, and the spread of plasmid-borne antimicrobial resistance genes [7](https://doi.org/10.1038/srep35617) [8](https://doi.org/10.7717/peerj.2595) [9](https://doi.org/10.3390/microorganisms9061201). Management today rests on universal pre-smolt vaccination, covert carrier screening by culture and PCR, and stress reduction during smoltification, with antibiotics reserved for confirmed outbreaks under susceptibility testing [1](https://doi.org/10.1111/1751-7915.12091) [2](https://doi.org/10.3389/fcimb.2025.1508135).
